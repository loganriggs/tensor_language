# Research update — 2026-09-02 18:49 UTC

## Current goal

The goal is to turn the bilinear transformer into a smaller set of named computations that can:

1. predict behavior on held-out examples and distributions;
2. be extracted as explicit computations;
3. be removed or changed without damaging unrelated computations;
4. be reused across examples, heads, layers, and tasks when they really implement the same operation; and
5. compose into an executable account of the model.

Parameter count, rank, quantization, and reconstruction error do not establish any of those properties by themselves.
The current route instead defines candidate pieces by exact model algebra and asks whether downstream computation
treats them the same way.

## What changed since the 18:19 update

The 18:19 update ended with one well-calibrated fact: replacing layer 8 head 4's matching score with layer 5 head
5's score restores the copy behavior, and MLP9 responds to that replacement much as it responds to the native score.
MLP9 does not respond similarly when we transplant the donor head's output/value content or use layer 7 head 3's
wrong-sign score.

Rung 501 used that known positive and negative to test all seven causally possible directed score replacements among
four equality-related head components: layer 5 head 5, layer 7 head 3, layer 8 head 3, and layer 8 head 4.

The purpose was not to rank heads. It was to ask whether the **score computation** from one head can replace the score
computation in another head while preserving both the copy-task effect and the downstream MLP9 response.

## The exact experiment

For a proposed donor `A` and later recipient `B`, the intervention kept `B`'s own attention output/value vectors but
replaced only its attention score with a scaled version of `A`'s score. In ordinary attention notation,

`output_B = sum_source softmax(score_B)[source] * value_B[source]`.

The score replacement changes the `softmax(score_B)` part and leaves `value_B` unchanged. A payload control did the
opposite: it kept the recipient's score but inserted the donor's output/value content. This is why a passing score
edge is more specific than two whole heads producing similar vectors.

Each proposed edge had to pass in two 250-document halves and in two backgrounds: with the earlier donor operating
normally and with that earlier donor removed. The fixed conditions were:

- restore between 65% and 140% of the native copy-task loss effect;
- match the per-document direction of that effect with cosine at least `.75` and residual at most `.70`;
- produce an MLP9 response with the same `.75/.70` conditions;
- beat the payload control clearly;
- affect copy positions more specifically than non-copy positions; and
- remain stable when the earlier donor was removed.

The run made exactly 24 scale-fitting forwards on the old fit set and 8,125 discovery forwards. Native replay was
bitwise exact, the attention-factor reconstruction error was `4.88e-14`, every intended edit was nonzero, and the
run took 176.5 seconds. Thus the result is a scientific failure to find a new edge, not a broken measurement.

## Result: only the known edge survives

![Rung 501 directed score results](assets/rung501_directed_score_graph.svg)

In the left panel, “direction match” is cosine multiplied by 100 for readability; it is not the percentage of the
effect recovered. Error bars show the range across the two document halves and two early-component backgrounds. The
right panel is the actual percentage of the copy-task effect restored.

The known `layer 5 head 5 score -> layer 8 head 4` relation was the only edge that passed every condition:

- copy-task recovery: `109.0%--121.1%`;
- copy-task effect cosine: `.897--.926`;
- MLP9 response cosine: `.822--.835`;
- MLP9 residual after fitting one positive scale: `.550--.569`;
- payload-control MLP9 cosine: only `.108--.123`; and
- copy-versus-noncopy MLP9 margin: `.207--.223`.

The response also stayed almost unchanged across the two early backgrounds: their MLP9 response directions had
cosine `.945/.949`, and the copy-effect recovery changed by only `.108/.046` in the two halves.

The registered negative control, `layer 7 head 3 -> layer 8 head 4`, remained strongly wrong-sign: its task cosine
was `-.896` to `-.911` and its MLP9 cosine was `-.788` to `-.795`.

## The informative near-candidate

`Layer 7 head 3 -> layer 8 head 3` initially looks like a new shared score computation:

- it restores `99.1%--100.7%` of the copy-task effect;
- its task cosine is `.948--.979`; and
- its MLP9 response cosine is `.821--.851`.

But the payload from layer 7 head 3 also gives a fairly similar MLP9 response: cosine `.650--.670`. In addition, one
copy-versus-noncopy margin is `.095`, below the fixed `.10` requirement. This pair may share a broad way of
perturbing MLP9, but the experiment cannot separate a shared copy-score computation from shared payload or generic
response. Calling it the same circuit would discard exactly the distinction we are trying to identify.

No other new candidate was close. We therefore keep one calibrated score relation rather than inventing a larger
graph from partial matches. This says that the current four-score set does not contain another relation satisfying
our full operational definition. It does not say that other heads cannot share computations, or that heads are the
correct final basis.

## Why the next experiment moves inside MLP9

The result strengthens the reason to stop treating whole heads as the final unit. We now have one typed upstream
change that MLP9 reliably recognizes. Because MLP9 is bilinear, we can exactly ask which earlier components it
multiplies to produce that response.

Immediately before MLP9, its residual input can be divided into 20 named contributions:

- the embedding/skip stream;
- attention outputs from layers 0 through 9; and
- MLP outputs from layers 0 through 8.

The block's learned residual-mixing coefficients are included, so these are the actual contributions reaching MLP9,
not unweighted old activations. After the shared RMS normalization, the MLP computes

`MLP9(z) = Down9[(Left9 z) * (Right9 z)] + bias`.

If `z = sum_s z_s`, substituting the 20 sources gives

`MLP9(z) - bias = sum_s sum_t Down9[(Left9 z_s) * (Right9 z_t)]`.

Combining the two orders for distinct sources leaves `20*21/2 = 210` unordered source pairs. For example, one term
might be “attention 5 output multiplied by attention 8 output,” and another might be “MLP0 output multiplied by
attention 8 output.” Their sum reconstructs the whole MLP9 output. A separate numerical term will retain the tiny
BF16/RMS-normalization remainder rather than pretending it belongs to a semantic source.

For the known score transplant, rung 502 will compute, for every pair `p`,

`pair response_p = pair output_p(recipient absent) - pair output_p(score transplanted)`.

Adding all 210 pair responses plus the numerical remainder must reproduce the full observed MLP9 response. This is
an exact decomposition of what changed locally, not an approximation and not a rank reduction.

## How gradients enter—and what they cannot prove

Running the complete model suffix separately for all 210 terms would be wasteful. On the discovery documents we can
differentiate copy-position CE with respect to the MLP9 output and calculate

`first-order pair effect = gradient dot pair response`.

We can perform the same contraction for the supported downstream circuit masks. This gives each exact pair a vector
describing which known downstream behaviors are sensitive to it. The shortlist must be stable across document halves,
must explain the score response more than the payload response, and must use at most 32 of the 210 pairs. All passing
pairs are retained; we do not choose whichever pair looks best after seeing confirmation data.

A gradient is only a local derivative. Earlier experiments showed that gradient-selected MLP products can predict a
code intervention yet fail on natural text. Therefore a successful rung 502 only identifies a candidate group. The
next required experiment removes that exact group from MLP9, runs layers 10--17 normally, and checks on held-out data:

- whether the group predicts and controls the copy effect;
- whether it works with the relevant partner component present and absent;
- whether payload and shifted-position controls fail;
- whether unrelated circuit metrics stay intact; and
- whether the same group works outside the examples used to choose it.

Only that finite test can promote the group from “exact local anatomy” to a circuit candidate. This directly follows
the project criteria: exact extraction, held-out prediction, selective removal, and reuse. It also implements the
interaction-focused direction in [`explanation_circuit_interactions.md`](../../../explanation_circuit_interactions.md)
rather than returning to rank reduction or compression-by-storage.
