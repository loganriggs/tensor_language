Eighteen Layers, Two Machines

The short version of what we now know about one small language model, from a program that demands a causal test, a control, and a null for every claim — and publishes its own corrections. Every term is defined where it's used.

The model, and the yardstick

bilin18
An 18-layer, 546M-parameter language model with two unusual choices: no softmax in attention (attention weights are a raw product of two matching-scores), and multiplication-based MLPs (each layer computes products of two linear views of its input). Trained on FineWeb prose.
the understanding score
For each part of the model we build a stand-in — a simple, human-legible replacement (a lookup table, a linear map, a fixed vector) — splice it in, and measure how much of the part's contribution to prediction survives. 0 = knowing nothing (replace the part with its average output), 1 = the real thing. All scores below are held-out: the stand-in is built on one half of the data and scored on the other, after we caught in-sample scores flattering themselves.

the organizing fact

The model is two machines sharing one wire

A grammar machine — the first two layers — works out each word's grammatical role (determiner, preposition, number, punctuation…) and what role should come next. A content machine — the deep middle — builds a running summary of what the text is about, and uses it to pick the specific word. Grammar is the easy quarter of the prediction problem; content is the hard three-quarters. The two write into nearly perpendicular directions of the same residual stream, and the same split appears in sibling models trained independently.

the first layer

You can't cut the grammar wire — but you can lie on it, and the model believes the lie

Class code: average the first MLP's output over every appearance of each word and the words sort themselves into grammatical clusters — determiners here, numbers there, punctuation in its own arm. The whole map can be computed from the weights alone, no text needed (agreement 0.83 with the data-derived map).

Deleting a class's code does almost nothing — the model keeps redundant copies (a parallel one in the first attention layer, plus the raw word re-injected at every layer, so downstream can always re-derive it). But transplanting — pasting a number's code where a determiner's code was — moves the model's next-word grammar toward what follows numbers, in 5 of 6 class pairs, with controls flat. The clusters are real causal variables; you just have to change their value rather than remove the wire.

attention, priced

The most important attention head is a constant — and most of attention is biases plus a crowd

The single costliest attention head (0.9 nats to delete — 8× the runner-up) turns out to add the same vector at every position of every text: it reads position 0, where an MLP manufactures a fixed vector, and broadcasts it. Replace the head with that one constant and 98.5% of its function survives. Across all 162 heads, 62% of individual-head value is bias (kept when the head's output is frozen to its own average) — yet freezing every head at once costs 5.7× the sum of the individual prices: the middle's pooling is a crowd that matters collectively while every member is individually expendable.

Nat: the unit of prediction loss (natural-log cross-entropy). This model's total loss on prose is ≈3.3 nats/token; 0.9 nats from one head is enormous.

the content machine's api

The "irreducibly high-dimensional" content splits into a skeleton and a scratch space

Skeleton: train a small sparse dictionary on the content signal and ~8 stable features fall out — the same 8 across retrainings (0.77 agreement) and largely the same in sibling models (7/8 across architectures). Several are human-readable: a livestock topic, a political-analysis register, a partitive construction ("some of / many of"). Scratch: the dense remainder, ~30% of the signal's variance, where feature identity dissolves.

The causal split is stark: every reader in the model reads the skeleton — filter the content down to the 8 features at any read point (deep layers, readout layers, the final step before the logits) and only 10–15% of the damage of full removal appears. The scratch is read by nothing — but it does real work in transit while the content is being built. Skeleton = the API; scratch = the workspace.

One earned caveat: the 8 features are individually nameable but not individually steerable — three different single-feature interventions all failed — and so does setting all eight at once to a donor's values. What has been demonstrated: patching a real passage's content pattern (a few dozen coordinates, at the matched point in the network) carries its topic to the destination. A first attempt to map this at finer grain failed its own positive controls and was withdrawn entirely — then the questions were re-asked inside the validated experiment, where the known result reproduced to the fourth decimal on fresh text. The valid answers: transport is position-bound — scramble which position holds which content vector and it is destroyed completely (worse than a random patch of equal size); a single passage-average vector carries nothing. A rank-8 slice already carries 78% of rank-16's effect. So the code is coarse in rank but strict in arrangement: topic lives in which content sits where. The mechanism: each position reads the topic written in its own coordinates almost completely (0.89 of the 0.90 full effect); neighbors receive only a small distance-decaying leak that respects the causal mask — patch the second half of a passage and the first half inherits exactly nothing. They behave like a population code: separately written, jointly causal, read locally at each position.

the frontier, budgeted

What we still can't tabulate is now a theorem, not a mystery: 53% + 17% + 30%

Simulating the content machine's construction — how the summary gets built layer by layer — decomposes exactly: 53% is plain linear transport of the state it starts from; 17% is what attention injects along the way; and the final 30% is created in flight by the nonlinear steps in layers 5–9. That last share is not hiding anywhere we failed to look: it is not linearly present in the starting state at any basis width, and not the multiplication's own quadratic shadow. To get it, you run the layers. Feature-level simulation caps at ~70%; the rest is computation, not representation.

behavior you can read

Turn off one machine and the failure is legible in the text itself

Free-running generation (the model writing its own continuations) under targeted ablations produces three different failure modes — judged by the intact model and by eye:

No content machine → function-word soup (grammar scaffolding, nothing on it):

far from the community the of the … by the, and the three a new at the way, a big, the proposed mid-

No grammar machine → fragment salad (content nouns survive, structure collapses):

: interactions)… 52, D. … Tribune. email@. Ingredients — Tribune Tribune-qu organisations

No value-residual (the channel broadcasting each word's fixed meaning-vector) → an unanchored stream: diverse contentful-looking tokens with zero repeated 4-grams in six thousand tokens:

As K DD ISI Pal Bag the and, His H and, and forward three Se arena

A matched control (two random middle layers ablated instead) writes near-normal text. And two independently-trained siblings (one a different MLP design, one half the width) reproduce the soup nearly verbatim in all three models and the zero-repetition stream exactly — the failure modes are properties of the family and its training data, not one model's quirks.

the scoreboard

Where the 90%-per-module goal stands — every number held-out

At or above 90%: layer-0 attention (literally a lookup table), the first three MLPs (0.93–0.95), the constant head (98.5%), the frequency-calibration knob (a single direction — remove it and calibration dies, remove a random direction and nothing happens). Ceiling-characterized (0.80–0.84): the transition MLPs and both readout MLPs — where two unrelated stand-in families converge on the same score, telling us the remainder is neither sparse nor linear. Bounded by results, not ignorance: the content construction (the 30% above) and the middle pool's two remaining unknowns, each closed with a documented list of every candidate explanation that failed.

Why "held-out" is underlined everywhere: the sweep that certified these numbers also caught the recurring failure mode — per-word lookup tables silently memorize rare words (a word seen once has its answer stored), inflating scores by up to 0.5. Fitted linear maps don't. Four earlier headline numbers were corrected downward in public when this was found.

how the sausage is made

The redteam caught the authors ~ten times, and every catch is in the ledger

Every experiment registers its predictions before running, with controls and nulls. The memorable saves: a "content decides the winner" claim killed by a norm-matched control (any same-size perturbation flips the winner); a compounding theory killed by its own discriminator experiment; an invariant "2.05× law" retracted when a third data point landed at 1.62; a whole four-experiment thread discovered to be a re-derivation of year-old results, prompting a per-module documentation system so it can't happen again. The published story only ever contains claims that survived their own attack.

the honest ledger

What we still don't understand — and what's been tried against each

1 · The created-in-flight 30%. A third of the content summary is manufactured by the nonlinear steps in layers 5–9. Tried: linear and nonlinear simulators, oracle attention inputs, quadratic features, three basis widths, direct maps — all cap at ~70%. It isn't hiding; it's computed. Update: the last escalation (a simulator whose frame rotates per layer) closes by argument — an earlier experiment already gave the simulator a strict superset of any such frame and gained nothing. "You have to run the layers" is final.

2 · Two attention unknowns, each with a documented graveyard: what the middle pool selects keys by (distance explains 58%; content-similarity and induction-matching both correlate but are causally inert), and — until a probe settled it — what drives each position's pooling amount. That second one just resolved: eight hand-crafted signals had failed (R² 0.09), but a small learned probe reads the amount almost perfectly (R² 0.93) from the position's content state — the gatherer pools hard while its running summary is thin and throttles as it fills; the earlier features were linear, the function isn't. And the key-selection criterion fell to the same trick days later: beyond its fixed recency kernel, each middle layer's attention is a state-controlled sharpening or flattening of the local window — two row-shapes carry half the remaining structure and their mixing reads out of the position's state at R² up to 0.88. Both of this band's "unknowns" were named by replacing hand-crafted features with learned probes — with one final twist that became a law of its own: plug those near-perfect probes back in as a generative model of the attention and they contribute nothing. What the probes read (the big, predictable part of the pattern) and what carries the loss (a fine query-key interaction residue) are different parts. Reading a signal and rebuilding its function are not the same thing.

3 · The readout's last fifth. Both output MLPs sit at 0.81–0.84 under two unrelated stand-in families — the remainder is neither sparse nor linear. Next (untried): a fitted low-rank quadratic, the third natural instrument family.

4 · A missing metric, not a missing fact — now a stated boundary: the grammar machine's generation failure is visible to any reader, but no word-class sequence statistic separates it from the content failure (bigrams and trigrams both tried: losing content words breaks class sequences just as badly). What a reader actually detects is the content-support asymmetry — structure without content vs content without structure — and that axis is already quantified. A parser-level metric would be the next escalation; out of scope here.
