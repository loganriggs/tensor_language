The model and the method

bilin18 is an 18-layer, 546M-parameter language model whose MLPs are purely bilinear — each one computes Down((Lx)·(Rx)), no activation function — and whose attention multiplies two separate score matrices with no softmax. The absence of nonlinearities beyond multiplication is what makes it readable: every layer is a third-order tensor, every output coefficient is an exact quadratic form, and sums split exactly, so decompositions that are approximations elsewhere are identities here.

Each layer went through the same loop. Find the causal directions by order-averaged attribution (each direction's damage averaged over all removal orders, so nothing hides behind redundancy and the parts must sum to the whole). Split each direction's activation exactly by writer — embedding, each attention block, each MLP. Name it two ways and keep only names that beat a permutation null. Replace it with the smallest surrogate that holds the model's loss, against a random-direction control. Then state the result as a variable graph and test each edge by transplanting values between inputs. Every claim below survived that loop, and the ones that didn't are reported with the same prominence.

The depth map

The single most useful picture. The ends of the network are individually heavy; the middle looks free one layer at a time — and is not. A fair, order-averaged attribution over the fourteen middle layers (green) shows two layers doing most of the middle's work, a long redundant tail, and one layer whose fair share is negative.

Layer 1 is the most important unit in the model (5.65 nats). Layers 2 and 3 carry 64% of the middle. Layer 4's negative share was the strangest number in the program until a coalition sweep oriented it: its cost flips sign exactly when layers 2–3 are deleted — layer 4 is a reader of layers 2–3, and on an unwritten bus its computation misfires. The tail (5–15) contributes 3–7% each, understated three-to-five-fold by one-at-a-time ablation.

unifying result These profiles turned out to be one measurement in disguise. For each layer, ask how much of its total deletion cost is captured by individual direction-bands plus pairwise band interactions: layers 1, 2, 3 score 24 / 39 / 57% — most of their effect exists only in third-order and higher combinations — while layers 0, 16, 17 score 67 / 99 / 100%. Every layer that compresses is interaction-shallow; every layer that resists is interaction-deep, in the registered ordinal prediction's exact order. Compressibility and interaction shallowness are the same property seen from two sides — which is this report's best one-line answer to what "understandable" means for a bilinear stack.

Two further regularities fall out. Compressibility tracks mechanism class: squared context summaries compress to one projection, token-identity features need more of their form, accumulated high-rank computation refuses entirely. And the distribution-robust part of every compressible leader is exactly its low-rank core: on out-of-distribution text the discarded parameters are neutral at best and harmful at worst — layer 16's replacement literally beats the intact model there, an effect that vanishes on training-like data (tested on a fresh fineweb sample).

The causal graph

The program's end state is not a list of features but a graph whose edges were each moved by hand: transplant a variable's value from one input into another and check the downstream behaviour matches transplanting its low-level realizer.

The z → c₀ abstraction inside layer 1 is honest about its grade: transplanted across documents it reproduces 68% of the true effect (against a 13% shuffled control) versus 92% on-distribution — the leak is localised to cross-document transport, exactly where the coefficient fit degrades. Partial abstractions with located leaks beat clean stories.

Structure verifies, token stories don't

Every time a claim of the form "direction X detects token-thing Y" met an actual intervention, it lost. Injecting layout characters into prose moves the supposed register-detector no more than injecting random words. Steering the syntax axis moves determiner probabilities less than it moves everything else. Meanwhile every structural claim — which head computes a variable, which layer feeds which, what gain an edge has, how few numbers replace a layer — survived intervention, three for three.

The reliable currency of this model's mechanisms is directions, edges, and gains — not token stories. The exact writer decompositions that suggested the token stories were not wrong as mathematics; they decompose covariance on the natural distribution, and covariance across documents is still correlational. An exact decomposition is not an intervention.

Edges are operators, and half a calculus works

The graph's edges began as scalars — a gain per edge — and scalars don't compose: knowing L0 feeds attention and attention feeds L1 predicted nothing about which head carries a given signal, because composition needs each edge's subspace signature. resolved The deepest structural question — whether the middle's dense interaction hides independent or mutually-exclusive sub-structure — resolved into a two-part answer, corrected twice along the way by its own controls. In support, the density is real everywhere: every downstream form and attention head couples essentially the same broad set of layer-1 direction pairs (one magnitude envelope, unsigned overlaps 0.6–0.8, at whole-model, per-reader, and per-form resolution). In functionals, the opposite: the signed quadratic forms the readers actually compute are nearly orthogonal — cosines 0.09–0.16 within readers, across readers, and across heads. The model reads its hardest layer through an overcomplete family of nearly-independent measurements on one shared substrate — and deleting direction-bands destroys every measurement over a region at once, which is exactly why the middle read as inseparable holism. The compression program re-aims accordingly: functional coordinates, not direction coordinates.

Two further structural facts came free. Edges are filters, not pipes: the same L0→L1 route passes the punctuation axis at unit gain, a second direction at 1.6σ, and the number axis at 0.23σ. And mediation itself is non-additive — single-head kill fractions sum past 100% on one edge and spread thinly on another — so "the" carrier is sometimes a category error, which is exactly what the failed blind test measured.

Composition has a law

Installing verified replacements together is where "did we compress it correctly" gets its final answer, and this model answered with structure. Three surrogates installed jointly cost 1.48× the sum of installing them alone — and the entire excess sits on the one pair of layers linked by a verified edge (the L16→L17 syntax bus; the unlinked pairs contribute +0.0005, zero within noise). The causal graph predicted, in advance and from a different kind of experiment, exactly where composition would fail.

refined A late-arc accounting correction sharpened these numbers: freezing the final norm's per-token gain to its no-damage values shows L17's raw span damage is 82% norm-mediated (the final rms_norm re-scaling a suddenly quieter residual; content-level cost +0.089, not +0.490) while L16's is mostly content (35% mediated) — and the interaction excess grows to +0.205 at content level, where it is ~90% the size of both individual content damages combined. The composition law was understated by the gain channel, not created by it. The full decomposition, measured stepwise: loss curvature alone (convex CE on superposing logit perturbations) would produce +0.46 of excess; the network's joint response destructively interferes and cancels about two-thirds of that; the remaining +0.16 content-level excess is carried ~21% by L17's quadratic and the rest by attention-and-upstream interaction. Compensation — the model actively attenuating compound damage — appears three separate times in this arc, and the product-law constant was always the residue of that cancellation war. The law's final scope, measured on a grid of damage shapes: it holds within a fixed damage family (where it was validated out-of-sample), fails across families (R² 0.12–0.47, as the bilinear-form refinement predicted), carries a content-level constant of ~13 rather than the norm-inflated 23, and inverts outright when one damage is small — the compensation machinery can make joint damage cheaper than the sum.

Three registered attempts to remove the interaction then failed in a row — refitting downstream-of-upstream closed 21%, raising the form rank closed 0%, widening the output span closed 30% — and the failures were the finding. Across all eight measured configurations the excess follows a product law:

excess ≈ 23 · d₁₆ · d₁₇   (c = 22.9 ± 2.4; 9% mean error)

A quadratic reader of summed errors produces a cross-term proportional to the product of the error magnitudes — the architecture's signature, now in the composition algebra. Every fidelity knob shrinks a factor; none removes the coupling, which is why no single fix could work. Tested out of sample at two never-measured corners with predictions registered first: 13% and 23% error, inside the registered 25% bar.

The practical rule for compressing this model: joint ≈ Σ dᵢ + c·Σ dᵢdⱼ over graph-linked pairs. The causal graph names which pairs interact; the law prices the interaction; compression budgeting becomes arithmetic.

refined A second linked pair (the 3→4 edge, probed with a grid of damage shapes) showed the scalar constant is per-damage-family, not per-edge: coupling strength varies ~5× with which directions are damaged. The full law is a bilinear form in the two damage profiles — the scalar version is its rank-1 shadow, valid when damage shape is held fixed, as it was in the validated 16→17 sweeps. The unlinked-pair control meanwhile confirmed the graph at its cleanest: no edge, no interaction, eighteen cells, excess ≤ 0.0013.

The circuit program

A second campaign, run at scale with parallel agents: instead of asking what each layer is, ask what each prediction uses. Clustering 54,000 predictions by which components' deletion hurts them gave 147 held-out-replicated "ownership circuits" covering 76% of the model's confident predictions — and three rounds of story-writing, red-teaming, and fresh-window confirmation taught us the honest certification unit: supervised function slices (induction, bracket-closing, digit continuation, subword completion, name continuation…), where ownership signatures replicate across document sets at cosine 0.90–1.00.

Four function circuits are causally certified (digit: attn8+mlp15; bracket-close: attn13; subword: mlp16+mlp15; name: attn1+attn0), and the mechanism findings came fast: the name circuit does its work at the antecedent (ablating its owners away from name sites destroys name predictions — they build the copy source); counting on synthetic prompts is literally the natural digit circuit (attn8 at +2.21 nats, ten times any other component, 100% accuracy); and the component certified as "net-harmful late attention" on average text (attn14) is the one that executes name retrieval when retrieval is the task.

The compression payoff: replacing the eight tail MLP span components with per-site-type constants — a ~0.7 KB description — recovers 50% of everything they do (55% with an agent-mined 34-class taxonomy); adding rank-8 input-reads for the two contextual classes reaches 95% (oracle-labeled) and 75% fully input-only. Attention splits into two materials: type-constant at structure sites (67–85% replaceable by per-class constants) and irreducibly contextual at copy sites (3%). And the front of the model folds: the first four MLPs are a token-dictionary cascade (68% / 85% / 58% / 44% token-determined), with the crown component mlp1 — the most important MLP in the model — 79% replaceable by a lookup table computed from the weights alone, one forward pass per vocabulary entry, no training data (a later measurement puts a hard price on the leftover: swapping these MLPs for pure per-token tables costs 1.0 nats at mlp0 and 1.8 at mlp1, so "mostly a dictionary" is a statement about variance explained, not about loss — the contextual remainder is small but expensive); layer 0's value broadcast is exactly lexical (a weights-computable table with literally zero error). Assembled, the certified stand-ins now replace thirty-four of thirty-six components — and iterating the assembly taught a composition grammar: token-conditioned tables act as error firewalls (lossy, stabilizing), input-conditioned stand-ins as error conductors (accurate solo, propagating in composition). A data audit then earned its own ledger entry: the standard eval window shares its corpus sample with the fit window and flattered every number by ~0.35 nats, so the frontier is now quoted on never-seen documents, with selection procedures validated out-of-sample. The honest ladder runs: front of the model at +1.98 nats, adding the six middle MLPs as weights-only truncations costs just +0.04 more (weights-derived stand-ins cannot overfit a window, so they travel; fitted tables and dictionaries pay a transfer tax — the old "incompressible middle" verdict was true only of input-generic replacements), tail attention dictionaries bring 27-plus components to +2.49, and the full 34-of-36 assembly lands at +2.93 nats on fresh text — 75% of the model's work retained, against an all-means floor that retains only 41% and an embeddings-only ceiling of +11.95. Attention's pattern side has its own census now: four named motifs (previous-token, self, induction-target, first-token) cover 85 of 162 heads across the depth, 71 of 74 named heads accept their literal one-sentence pattern for under a hundredth of a nat individually — though jointly the swaps conduct error and compound, the same composition problem the MLPs posed. Three closing structural results, all replicated on never-seen text: the weight matrices themselves are entrywise Gaussian (kurtosis 0.03–0.52, sparsity and privileged coordinates at exact chance) — every structure in this model lives in subspaces and functions, never in entry statistics; the "attention writes, adjacent MLP reads" handoff is causally concentrated in exactly three junctions (blocks 0, 1 and 5, at +0.96, +1.95 and +2.16 nats, all other blocks ≈ free); and the largest of these — attn5 to mlp5 — is a four-direction channel that range-regulates the long-mysterious private 8-dim code (cutting it makes that code's variance explode 4.3× while neighboring directions go quiet), a regulatory function carried by the channel's average — which is exactly why the assembly can replace that whole attention layer with a single mean vector and still preserve the code's statistics. The assembly campaign's closing discovery is an economics: replacing components is priced not by each stand-in's own accuracy but by the local fidelity of the stream at the sites where downstream rungs read — an 18% local stream difference amplified a later rung's cost 2.4×, while global stream error near 100% coexists with only +1.4 nats of behavioral cost (the model's output is extraordinarily robust to internal substitution, so a working assembly never implies close streams). Weights-derived "fold" tables — every vocabulary token run alone through the real prefix, zero training data — beat fitted tables on both windows and carried the best low-coverage point: 20 of 36 components at +1.46 nats on never-seen text (88% of the model's work), with the honest coverage-fidelity envelope running 20 @ +1.46, 20 components + 38 one-sentence attention heads @ +2.29, and 34 @ +2.93 — a genuine trade-off curve with a measured mechanism, not an unfinished backlog. The circuit census then scaled by an order of magnitude in a single day through a recursive "data-dual" instrument: fingerprint each token by which causal perturbations hurt it, factor the token-by-probe damage matrix into covariance modes, and recursively refine probes inside the implicated components (slice-conditioned output directions, individual heads), gating every node on replication across disjoint documents so the algorithm refuses to invent structure it cannot reproduce. Result: 118 supervised circuits (from 4 certified a day earlier), 91 of them invisible to the hand-built ten-class taxonomy, with a quantitative naming protocol — a blind grader must pick a circuit's members out of a shuffle given only its name — that certified 47 of 115 names as genuinely concept-transmitting. An adversarial audit then scoped the claim precisely: the census survives distinctness, contrastiveness, and confound attacks cleanly, but joint ablation shows that single-component fingerprints do not compose into predictable response directions — the model's holistic composition physics at circuit scale — so these are certified causal-dependence neighborhoods (data that specially depends on specific components, moving far more than matched controls), not sign-predictable linear response units; true linear units exist as a minority subpopulation. The downgrade was triggered by its own pre-registered stakes, and the reshaped claim then survived its own fresh test — twenty untouched circuits, 75% passing the registered selectivity bar (members moving at least twice as much as matched controls under joint ablation of their components). Three adversarial attacks repelled, one landed and reshaped the claim, and the reshaped claim replicated: the census's final, certified form. The overnight cash-in then delivered three results worth their own sentences. Two circuits reached full induction grade — mechanisms read off the weights alone (all corpus token-pairs run through the model's front) that predict their own members at 33–36× base precision, and readable: one fires on the second piece of rare multi-token words. A sign-calibrated per-position ablation policy derived from the census improved the untouched model on never-seen text while a random-position control hurt it — the program's first interpretability-guided edit that beats the model it explains. And the certification pipeline was calibrated against five purpose-built fakes: fabricated, confounded, and adversarially statistic-selected circuits are all rejected by three hard dimensions (selectivity, sign-mixedness, structural matching), with one blind spot — unions of real circuits — proven invisible to per-candidate statistics and governed instead by the census tree's own bookkeeping. The census's final headline is a row of measurements, each with registered bars, controls, and fresh-data legs: 118 replicated circuits / 35 corpus-general / 47 blind-nameable / 14 capturable by 64-bit token programs / 4 at full induction grade / 16.7% linear response units (Wilson 95%: 9–28%) — with nameability and linearity shown to be independent axes (a registered association test, refuted), and the value of causal circuit knowledge over surface descriptions priced directly: position-gating with causal labels buys nats at 9.4× the rate of random gating, while the best input-only 64-bit programs manage 1.5× — that seven-fold gap being, in nats per token, exactly how much of what the census knows lives above the surface-feature class. A compositional ladder then raised the programmable count the bottom-up way: certified circuits become predicates for the next round's rule search (attention layer 0 is exactly lexical and 27 certified previous-token heads transport content one position back, so every feature legally gains shifted copies). Iterated until dry, the ladder converges at 57 of 118 programmable — programs are literally circuits-of-circuits — but the growth is one multiplicative step (+63%), not a cascade, and two registered structural predictions about the resulting call-graph failed informatively: composition edges do not follow physical depth (48% layered, a coin flip), and no circuit is cited by more than three others. When one circuit's program cites another, it borrows a correlated context family, not a layered subroutine — the data-space echo of the weights-side finding that these heads share pattern types but no common OV library. Scaling the deploy gate to all 57 programs multiplied the absolute benchmark gain twelve-fold (+0.17 nats on fresh text, the largest deploy-legal move so far) while the efficiency ratio stayed at 1.5× random: the deploy gap is a property of the description language, not of how many programs are written in it. Three further arcs then closed in quick succession, and together they name what kind of object this model's circuits are. First, the description-language limit is now measured across six feature classes — surface predicates, token classes, trigger pairs, exact weight folds of 1- and 2-token context, unbounded-range match transports, and early-stream projections — and the programmable count never leaves 55–58 of 118: for the residual half, membership is not position-definable in any language tried; those circuits are activation-space objects. Second, the intervention algebra: across PCA bases, gradient-learned bases, and natural values on ten circuits, only subtraction bites — interventions act by removing variance along directions (with a measurable off-manifold surcharge for zeroing), and the written value is causally void; a learned "steering" direction turned out to produce identical effects whether one wrote the optimized value or zero. Value-patching methodology from ReLU-transformer interpretability does not port. Third, mechanism: the model does IOI at 99% with a genuine mover head — yet that head is a general repeated-structure head that specializes in context, the IOI owners compose as a parallel sum (every joint ablation lands at 97–99% of the sum of singles), and the textbook induction algorithm, implemented literally as code, is refuted outright (+9.8 nats, correlation 0.03): the "induction heads" are opportunistic part-time match-readers whose faithful code must include the double-QK pattern computation itself. Every circuit measured is also two-signed — a policy helping some members and fighting others — while remaining corpus-wide net-useful (10/10 audited), the member-level harm being pure tail-selection. The census, on this evidence, is a targeting system: it says where dependence concentrates; the computation it points at is dense, contextual, additive, and native to the bilinear algebra rather than to sparse human-legible rules. And this structure is a family trait, not a quirk: the 12-layer sibling shows the same anatomy — front junctions, a free tail, and one narrow mid-depth junction (top-4 directions carry 85%) whose channel lives inside its private code's subspace (overlap 0.66 vs a 0.01 floor) with the same class-damage signature down to the negative-newline quirk. One conserved complex — a mid-depth private code with a ~4-direction service channel beside its fraction-⅓ writer — with the plumbing order (regulate from upstream vs carry forward from downstream) as the only parameter that varies between models. The larger-checkpoint preregistration now carries this as a registered expectation alongside the writer-depth law. Weight structure sharpened the recipe twice over: because a bilinear MLP is a sum of rank-1 quadratics, keeping half of each middle layer's hidden units — with zero fitted parameters — reproduces the middle well enough that the 19-component assembly lands at +1.68, and quadratic absorber features derived from each layer's own read-directions independently cut the all-fitted version to +1.99. A correction earned its own ledger entry here: the norm-product ranking we first used to pick the kept units turned out to add nothing — selection nulls showed a random subset does as well, and the bottom three-quarters beats the "top" quarter at all six layers. The truncated stand-in works because the middle is massively redundant, not because importance is readable off the weights (new standing rule: no selection claim without its selection null). Two further structural hypotheses failed their registered bars and are recorded as negatives: the middle layers do not read a low-dimensional input subspace, and restricting error-correction to a 64-dim "what downstream reads" interface subspace made the assembly worse — the middle's interfaces are broad.

instrument check The campaign's main tool asks a cheap correlational question — which upstream component's contribution is enriched in a circuit's input — and we tested whether its answer predicts the expensive causal one. Across the components of one circuit the enrichment ratio and the damage caused by actually ablating each component rank-correlate at 0.76 (0.84 before excluding components that are the circuit's own machinery, where the correlation is partly definitional). So the table ranks magnitude usefully.

What it does not do is isolate a culprit, and running it at scale said so twice over. Across 60 certified circuits only three showed any stable enrichment; of those, one reproduced on unrelated circuits (a layer property) and two were genuinely specific — the same writer feeding two sibling circuits. We then ablated that writer: it damages those circuits' positions at concentration 4.3, and the neighbouring component the table does not flag damages them at 4.4 and 4.9. The same pattern held for the other confirmed case. So in this model, input-composition analysis ranks how much a component matters but cannot separate a writer from its neighbours, and the honest census yield is: most circuits here have no concentrated upstream source at all.

Honest negatives from the same campaign: the naive match-and-copy stand-in for the induction band was refuted twice (the copy is distributed, not a single-position value injection); the model cannot do single-digit addition; and early story-writing rounds taught, at the cost of two ledger-style corrections, that mechanical certification bars admit gerrymandered stories — the standing protocol is now red-team review plus never-seen-window confirmation.

The model on readable code

The newest campaign raised the bar from describing parts to replacing their computation with short programs you could read aloud — and measuring exactly what that costs. The first circuit to close at this grade is induction. Each induction head, it turns out, effectively reads from only a handful of positions: keeping just its four strongest-scoring reads per position reproduces 98.8% of what the head does (two reads give 96%; the single strongest carries 77%). The head's entire program is one line — "sum the four largest coincidence-scored reads" — and it passed a preregistered replication bar six-fold over, with a shuffled control broken. Two prettier hypotheses died on the way: the extra reads are not additional copies of the match (only 20% of the dropped attention sits on the match family), and there is no context-corruption effect.

The same one-line code was then priced across the whole stack: 120 of the 162 attention heads run on it at negative cost — the code is slightly better than the real head — and 140 fit within +0.08 nats. The MLP half of the architecture has a matching sparse form: each bilinear MLP is a sum of 4,608 rank-one quadratic units, and keeping the top 512 per position costs just +0.044 nats on never-seen text, while a random 512 costs 22× more. Put together — four-line code on the 120 cheapest heads, top-512 units in every MLP — the whole model runs in a form where every position's computation is an enumerable list, at +0.22 nats on fresh text (and the IOI benchmark actually improves). One caveat matters and was measured: the selection is routed, not fixed. The 512 most-used units capture only 18% of the per-position slots, and freezing a static 512 costs 9× the dynamic version. The model's MLP semantics live in the routing function, not in a small fixed subnetwork.

The input side of induction then closed exactly. The score that decides where an induction head looks compares, at every pair of positions, the same quantity: what the first MLP wrote there. That MLP is the model's identity-code generator — a token-enrichment table that all match machinery compares — and its writer pair dominates the coincidence score of all nine induction heads. For the layer-1 head the story is now complete and mechanical: taking the token embedding plus the first MLP's actual write (and the deterministic rotary rotation), and nothing else, predicts the head's strongest read at 99.8% accuracy with rank correlation 0.999. The attention layer below contributes only through that MLP's input — its own residual write is irrelevant to the pattern. Purely token-computable versions (folding the MLP into a lookup table) reach about two-thirds.

Chasing the deeper heads' gap closed the whole story. Rebuilding the residual with every attention write deleted and every real MLP write kept showed the early heads match on that MLP-enriched code alone (86% and 74% at layers 2–3) — and what the deeper heads add turned out to be the same code, moved. Head 5.5's missing content arrives almost entirely through attention layer 4; recomputing that layer's write with its real attention patterns but values read from the pure MLP-ladder residual recovers 95% of the effect. Layer 2's head closes at 99.9% the same way. The deepest head studied (8.4) needs one more twist: a one-shot relay reaches only 62%, but letting relays compound — attention moving code that was itself already moved — reaches 95% of the real channel, while a shuffled-value control falls below baseline. So the induction machinery of this model compares one identity code: written by the embedding, enriched layer-by-layer by the MLP chain, and moved between positions by attention — deep heads read relayed, even doubly-relayed, copies — with each head then executing its four strongest coincidence reads. One qualification the window experiments added later: the band is mutually covering. Restricting any single induction head to a four-token window costs essentially nothing at repeat positions (mean 0.003 nats across the nine, and four of them are slightly helpful), while restricting all nine together costs 0.318 — a hundred-fold superlinearity, against tenfold for nine random heads. So "four reads of an identity code" describes what the band computes collectively; no individual head is necessary, and the distant reads are what matters (windowing the nine recovers 75% of the damage that deleting them outright causes). The bound is quantitative: allowing the code one move closes the early heads (94–100% pattern agreement), two moves close every head to 93%+, three to 98%+. And the claim is causal, not just predictive: running the live model with all nine induction heads' patterns computed from this reconstruction costs −0.002 nats at match positions — indistinguishable from intact — while a shuffled-content control costs +0.07 and deleting the heads costs +0.50.

The census that targets this work got its own audit. Rebuilt on a five-times larger, deliberately diverse training-distribution corpus (at most two rows per document), it yields 311 damage-mode leaves and no leaf is a disguised document detector (median top-document share: 1%). But the partition is not a stable ontology: trees rebuilt in different contexts re-carve the same data (9% member identity, 40% containment), so a circuit's identity is its machinery and causal profile, with member lists as evidence. That rule is now enforceable: computing each leaf's damage profile independently on disjoint corpus halves, 77% of leaves replicate (profile cosine 0.96 matched vs 0.54 mismatched) — 88% at coarse grain, 56% for the finest leaves. A correction earned within hours of that number: the raw-damage cosine turns out to certify stability of magnitude, not selectivity — the leaves it ranks highest are whole-component probes whose damage is big, global, and therefore trivially stable, and they fail a local- selectivity gate at five times the rate of uncertified leaves. The working certification standard is now selectivity replicated on both halves independently — and under that standard the depth profile inverts: fine-grained leaves pass at 72% versus 44% for coarse ones, so the census's fine structure is real, locally selective, corpus-stable machinery; only its raw damage magnitudes are unstable. The production shortlist stands at 199 of 311 leaves, with a label-shuffled null at 6%. A second correction followed from a reviewer's objection: because these positions already run high loss, we asked what an arbitrary ablation of the same size would score. A rank-matched random subspace in the same components reaches a median concentration of 2.62 — just under the gate of 3 — and only a third of leaves reach twice their random baseline. Leaf selectivity is real but sits on a large fragility baseline: a leaf scoring 5 is roughly 1.7× an arbitrary ablation, not 5× nothing. The numbers stand; the old reading of them does not.

The most expensive head is a constant

Deleting attention heads one at a time across all 162 of them turns up an outlier that is hard to miss: head 7 of layer 5 costs 0.92 nats to remove — eight times the next head, and more than most whole-layer ablations in the depth map above. Roughly a quarter of the model's heads, spread evenly across depth, cost nothing at all.

What that head does turned out to be simple, and slightly absurd. It reads position 0 — the first token — for 99.8% of queries, where a neighbouring head reads it 5% of the time; and the value parked at position 0 has a norm of 730 against 197 everywhere else. So the head adds nearly the same vector at every position. Testing that directly: replacing its entire output with its own mean vector costs −0.005 nats — free, in fact a hair better than leaving it alone — and a mean borrowed from unrelated text works just as well. The single most important attention head in this model is a learned constant: 1152 numbers, no text dependence.

Chasing the constant upstream closed the circuit end to end. The vector head 5.7 broadcasts is written at position 0 by the fourth MLP, whose output there has a norm of 155,000 against 14,800 at ordinary positions — ten times larger — and whose direction is essentially the same in every document we tested (mean pairwise cosine 0.998, and still 0.998 between texts that begin with different tokens). Feeding that position's value through the head's own output projection reproduces the head's average write at cosine 0.999. So the full mechanism, with every part named and no approximation between them: the MLP chain manufactures a fixed vector at the first position, one head is dedicated to reading that position, and its job is to add that vector everywhere.

And the constant turns out to be the stream itself. Comparing it against the residual at every layer: it sits at cosine 0.99 with the mean residual direction through layers 6–11, and it accounts for 62–72% of the residual's magnitude at layers 6–8 (falling to 0.27 cosine by the final layer, where the network turns toward producing logits). Against a random-vector baseline of 0.017, the alignment is fifty-fold. So after layer 5 the residual stream is mostly one fixed vector, with everything the text actually says riding on top as a smaller perturbation. That explains the rest of the measurements at a stroke: rotating the constant to a random direction of the same length costs 6.3 nats — seven times worse than deleting it — because it moves the centre the whole stack is calibrated around, while halving or doubling it costs almost nothing, and no local ablation finds where its value lives (the parts sum to a quarter of the 0.92-nat whole) because being the baseline is not a local property.

and the first layer is a table Chasing the second costliest head (+0.11 nats) led somewhere cleaner. At layer 0 attention can only read token embeddings, so a head there is in principle a lookup — and in practice it is: replacing head 0.3's attention pattern with one computed from tokens and positions alone, and its values with a 50,304-entry per-token table, costs zero measurable loss, while the same table with token identities shuffled costs +0.15. Doing this to all nine heads of layer 0 at once also costs zero (shuffled: +0.24). The boundary is sharp: the identical construction applied to layer 1 — which reads layer-0 outputs rather than raw tokens — costs +1.47 nats, a hundred and fifty times more. So the first attention layer of this model is exactly a bigram table, the second is not, and the line between them is measurable to two decimal places.

How much context does that front end actually need? With the first attention layer rebuilt from weights and its reads restricted to a sliding window, the answer is startlingly small: giving the first MLP the current token plus one previous position costs 0.004 nats against the full model, and a four-position window is free (−0.04). Only cutting to the current token alone hurts (+0.54). So the entire front of this model — first attention layer and first MLP together — is a bigram function, exactly computable from two tokens, and we can say that with the untruncated reconstruction verified exact at +0.0000.

how far does this model look? Restricting one attention layer at a time to a four-token read window prices how much each layer needs distant context. Seventeen of eighteen layers cost under a tenth of a nat. The single exception is layer 5 at +1.11 — and that turns out to be this same sink losing its constant: allow position 0 through the window and the cost collapses to +0.08, while windowing head 5.7 alone reproduces half the damage and windowing the other eight heads of that layer costs +0.05. So on average text, every genuine content read in this eighteen-layer model fits inside four tokens, plus one constant fetched from the first position.

Two things make this more than a curiosity. First, this architecture has no softmax, so the usual story for attention sinks — a place to dump normalized attention mass — cannot apply here; the sink exists to deliver a bias. Second, it is nearly unique: sweeping all 162 heads, only one other head has its cost fully absorbed by a constant, and across the twelve heads that cost more than 0.02 nats the median share explained by a constant is 39%. Bias-adding is not a widespread role in this model — it is one enormous special case sitting on top of an otherwise contextual stack.

Three readings that died

One thread started as a circuit claim and ended somewhere better. Several independently discovered damage-clusters shared a behaviour: ablating their machinery improved the model's predictions at punctuation. Three rounds of controls dismantled the obvious readings — it is not specific to any one cluster (a component belonging to none of them reproduces it), and it is not explained by punctuation simply being frequent (within the top frequency quartile punctuation still helps 78% against 54% for everything else).

What it is not. Our first reading was that the model over-continues at phrase boundaries — at helped positions its top choice is a non-punctuation continuation 75% of the time. That figure was computed at positions selected because ablation helped there, and the unselected measurement refutes it: across all 1,602 punctuation targets in unseen text, the model's top choice is a non-punctuation token only 23.5% of the time. It gets punctuation right three times in four. We published the selected number before running the unselected one; this paragraph replaces it.

What it is. Ablation damages punctuation predictions less than everything else, and that part replicates on unseen text under both interventions: the 16-dimensional bundle gives −0.010 nats at punctuation against +0.015 elsewhere, and ablating five whole components gives +0.325 against +0.746. Both spare punctuation; they differ only in severity. So there is a real, reproducible dissociation — it is just a claim about robustness, not about a bias, and it is much less exciting than what we first wrote down.

Two more readings then died. Predictability does not explain it: matching positions on the intact model's confidence leaves 91% of the sparing intact, and damage turns out to be roughly flat across confidence deciles rather than falling. And the effect is not universal damage either — a random subspace of the same size in the same components produces the opposite sign at punctuation (+0.007), while ablating real machinery of three different kinds produces sparing (bundle −0.025, one attention head −0.008, one MLP −0.062).

Where it ends: ablating real machinery in this model shifts competence away from ordinary words and toward punctuation and digits; arbitrary perturbations of the same size do not. Five readings were tested and four died — a circuit claim, a frequency claim, a selection-driven bias claim, and a predictability claim — and the finer class profile turns out to depend on which machinery you remove (newlines, for instance, are damaged by one intervention and spared by another). The surviving statement is narrow and true, which is the most this particular thread supports. Its real yield was methodological: never compute a rate at positions selected by the outcome you are measuring, and register a margin rather than a sign.

A head that only cares about line breaks

Every circuit in this program until now was found by asking which components are expensive to delete. That question has one answer for every behaviour — the front of the model — because the front does everything. Ranking a component by how concentrated its damage is on one behaviour, relative to its own damage everywhere else, asks a different question and gives a different answer.

Applied to line breaks: attention layer 12 costs 0.078 nats at positions where the next token is a newline and 0.007 everywhere else. The component that led the old magnitude ranking, by contrast, is ten times more important away from line breaks than at them. Layer 12 is also not simply a layer that matters late in a sequence: shifting the target positions six tokens in either direction cuts its advantage by a factor of 2.7.

Nine heads live in that layer, and one of them carries 88% of the effect. Deleting head 12.6 costs 0.068 nats at line breaks, 0.006 at the shifted control positions, and nothing at all in ordinary text. What makes this more than a lucky search is that the head was named in advance: a general-purpose profile of all 162 heads, built with no knowledge of newlines, records deletion costs of 0.008 or less for eight of layer 12's heads and 0.073 for 12.6. Predicting the newline head from that profile alone could have failed eight ways.

What the head does is narrow to the point of being a single number. Deleting it lowers the model's score for the newline character by 0.137 while the strongest competing token moves by 0.004 — a factor of thirty. It is not making line breaks broadly easier to predict; it is moving one token, and it moves it in both directions:

It also reads the document, not just the token. Holding the trigger token fixed, the push is 2.1× larger in documents that are already full of line breaks — verse, lists, tables, chat logs — than in flowing prose. So it has two inputs: a local one (what just ended) and a global one (is this the kind of text that breaks lines at all).

One hypothesis died cleanly along the way. Regularly-structured text breaks lines at a predictable interval, so a head could predict the next break by counting tokens since the last one. It doesn't: breaks that match the document's own median line length cost 0.0875 when the head is removed, and breaks that don't cost 0.0909. Identical. Whatever it is computing, it is not arithmetic on line length.

Where its two inputs come from took two attempts. Silencing each upstream component's contribution to the head's query found nothing: no single source worth more than 0.015 of AUC, though removing all of them at once cost 0.14 — more than twice the sum of the individual losses. I first read that as a diffuse input. It is better read as a consequence of the architecture: with no softmax and no activation function, an attention score here is a product of two bilinear forms, and removing one contributor at a time is a first-order probe of a multiplicative object. It will report "nothing matters alone, everything matters together" whether or not the underlying structure is simple.

Testing the head's other two input paths found what the first attempt missed. On the key and value paths, one component — attention layer 10 — moves the document-level gating by 48% of its full size, against 7% for everything the query side could manage. Detector quality on those same paths is carried mostly by the token embedding (−0.098 of AUC, nine times the best contextual source), so the head's reading of "this looks like a line end" is largely lexical while its sense of "this document breaks lines" is layer 10's contribution. Silencing all context on the key path alone doesn't shrink the gating — it reverses it.

Following that thread to the end simplified the head further and corrected two of my own intermediate claims. The query barely varies from one closing bracket to the next: replacing it with a single fixed vector — the average query over bracket positions — reproduces the head at zero cost, and reproduces its full distance profile bin by bin. So the head does not compute a distance and does not adapt to one (an earlier version of this section said it did); it applies one constant "attend to a bracket opener" query everywhere. What makes that query land on the right opener is the architecture. With no softmax, the attention score is a product of two bilinear forms — a soft-AND — and here both factors turn out to be position-driven: token identity makes bracket openers stand out from ordinary text (the candidate pool), and rotary, redundantly in both factors, picks the opener at the query's fixed relative offset. Because that offset prefers recent positions and nesting is rare, the chosen opener is almost always the true match — which is exactly why nested brackets defeat it. The head is a fixed-query, token-gated, positional AND, and that is simpler than every story I told on the way to it.

Layer 8 was already implicated in digit prediction by a completely different method — a sixteen-dimensional subspace found through damage clustering, whose removal makes digits harder by 0.135 nats across five disjoint samples while removing comparable subspaces makes them easier. That last coincidence looked like one component tracking document structure generally. It isn't. Where an attention head keeps its decision

The bracket head and the newline head do unrelated jobs — one points at the opener a closing bracket belongs to, the other decides a line break. Both turned out to compute their decision the same way, and finding that out relied on a fact about this architecture: the query side of an attention head is entirely linear. The query is rotary(rms_norm(W·x)), and rms-norm is just a per-position scalar while rotary is a rotation, so the query is an exact linear image of the residual. That means the contribution of every upstream component to the head's selection can be computed by composing weights, with no ablation and no approximation — reconstructed here to one part in ten million.

Composed that way, the selection at first looks hopeless: about a dozen late-layer components each contribute 6–9% of the query, none dominant, and removing the common-mode part doesn't concentrate it. No single upstream component sets the bracket distance.

But "spread across many writers" and "spread across many directions" are different claims. Projecting directions out of the head's query input — the eigenvectors of the selection gradient — tells a different story:

The subspace is sharply behaviour-specific: projecting it out costs 30–60× more at the positions where the behaviour happens than a random subspace does, and at other positions it costs essentially nothing (for the newline head, literally zero).

The decision variable isn't a vague distributed mess; it's a named subspace. That turns out to be a general property of this model, and it cuts the other way from what "trace it to the embedding" would hope. Asking what writes into a selection subspace, exactly and one layer at a time, the answer is: about a dozen components, none dominant, depositing into it at all positions rather than only where the behaviour occurs. There is no sparse upstream circuit to follow back. The same split shows up wherever this model spreads a computation — induction is a band of nine heads that individually do almost nothing (108× superadditive), and the digit signal spans four heads. Yet in every case the read side stays compact: the digit effect, across its four heads, concentrates so that a single direction carries more than half of it.

So the honest shape of this model is one fact seen from two sides: behaviour-relevant signals are written into compact direction-subspaces by many diffuse sources. The read side is low-rank and findable; the write side is distributed and does not decompose into nameable parts. That is why the selection subspaces are recoverable but their upstream origins are not — and it means the clean, fully-traceable circuits in this model are the exception (attention layer 0, the front-block tables, the three structural heads), sitting alongside a large remainder that is real, measurable, and genuinely distributed.

One thing it is not, at least so far, is shared. The bracket and newline selection subspaces overlap at a cosine of 0.20 against a chance floor of 0.12 — a direction or two in common, not a reused variable. On the two circuits looked at, each head computes its own private decision subspace. Whether some signal further back is genuinely reused across circuits is still open, but it isn't these.

Working both heads all the way down gave a general account of how selection is done here, and it turns on the architecture again. With no softmax, an attention score is a product of two bilinear forms — a soft-AND — and both heads apply a roughly fixed query and let that AND do the selecting. What the AND resolves is where the two heads part ways, and it's set by what the task needs rather than by the mechanism:

  • The bracket head must identify which opener a closing bracket belongs to, so token identity gates the pool of openers and position (through rotary, redundantly in both factors) discriminates within it. That's why it fails on nested brackets — the positional preference is fixed at "recent," which is only the true match when nothing is nested.
  • The newline head only needs to detect that recent line-break structure exists, so it attends to newline tokens by identity and is completely indifferent to which newline — given two, it shows no preference at all.

So the general shape is: a fixed query picks out a token class through the double-QK AND, and the head either resolves a specific member by position (matching) or attends to the class indifferently (detection). Getting the bracket head to this point took walking back several intermediate stories of my own — matcher, then adaptive pointer, then distance-computer — each refuted by a sharper test; the honest endpoint is the simplest of them.

That fixed-query story does not cover every head, and finding where it breaks gave a second mechanism class. An induction head — one that predicts a repeated token by finding where the current token last appeared and copying what followed it — cannot use a fixed query, because it has to encode the current token to know what to match. Testing that directly: the digit head's real per-position query attends to the position after a prior occurrence at 6.4× a control, and replacing it with a single averaged query collapses that to under a third. Content matching lives in the query; fixed-query selection does not.

So the model runs at least two distinct attention mechanisms, and one measurement tells them apart — does a single fixed query reproduce the head?

  • Fixed-query selection. A constant query names a token class; the double-QK AND and rotary do the rest. Positional when the task needs a specific referent (brackets, quotes), detection when it needs only class presence (newlines). The query carries no per-position information.
  • Content induction. A per-position query encodes the current token and matches its earlier occurrence, attending to the successor. The query is the mechanism; a fixed query destroys it.

How much of the model is which? Replacing every head's query with a single fixed average, one head at a time, is nearly free for 161 of 162 heads — the exception costs 0.04 nats. Doing all of them at once costs 0.96 nats, about 29% of the loss, against 10.3 for random queries. So the model's attention is dominated by fixed-query selection: fixing the entire query computation lands within about one nat of the real model, eleven times closer than a random baseline. The content-query remainder — induction and the rest — is worth that residual nat, spread across many heads that each cost almost nothing alone (the cost is superadditive by nearly four times, the same non-composition seen everywhere else in this model), and it is front-loaded: the first six layers carry 59% of it. Content matching is real but it is a distributed minority of what attention here does.

How small can the front of the model get?

The point of finding circuits is a benchmark: replace parts of the model with interpretable computation and see whether the outputs survive. The early layers are where that should be easiest, because the embedding folds in exactly and every truncation below is an SVD of a weight matrix — a derivation, not a fit.

The folding really is exact. Block 0 forms a scalar multiple of the token embedding and rms_norm is scale-invariant, so attention layer 0's input is the token's own embedding, verified to 7×10⁻⁸. Everything that layer computes is a function of token identity and distance, with no context and no approximation.

And it is astonishingly narrow. Truncating every head's query and key maps to rank 2 — two directions out of 128 — costs 0.053 nats. Random projections of the same rank cost 1.35, and random projections of rank 64 still cost 1.28. So this is not robustness to damage: the right two directions are nearly free and an arbitrary sixty-four are catastrophic. Reading off what those directions score highest, with no data involved, gives morphology — 'ed' 'ing' 'ers' 'ating' on the query side, 'ates' 'ations' 'izing' on the key side — which is what a layer that is provably an exact bigram table ought to be comparing.

The MLP beside it is a different animal. It writes into 64 of 1152 directions for under 0.10 nats but needs 256 to read through, and both sides beat the fitted per-token lookup table (1.466 nats on the same text) from rank 8 onward. Reading the embedding through eight directions already does better than a full vocabulary table.

Every layer individually looks like it has slack; the slack at any one layer is apparently being used by some other layer. One more result worth its own line, because it contradicts what the weights appear to say. The architecture re-injects the token embedding into every block at strength 8 against roughly 1 for everything written below, which looks like the embedding dominating the computation. Layer 0 has already written the token information into the stream, and the eightfold re-injection is redundant. Coefficient magnitude in the residual is not information flow.

Splitting one layer's 4608 pieces into reusable groups

Every circuit so far in this report was found one piece at a time: pick a component, test whether removing it hurts. The first MLP layer alone has 4608 internal pieces (its "hidden units" — each one a small quadratic computation on the input), far too many to test one by one. This section asks a different question: instead of testing pieces individually, can pieces that tend to matter together — removing one hurts the same sentences that removing another one hurts — be grouped automatically, and do those groups turn out to mean something?

The method: for many real sentences, remove each piece one at a time and record which sentences got worse. Two pieces that damage the same sentences are probably doing related work, even if nothing about their raw numbers looks similar. Group pieces by that pattern of shared damage, then check whether a group holds together on sentences it has never seen before (the honest test — a fake grouping wouldn't reproduce on fresh data), and read what a group actually responds to by looking at real sentences where it fires hardest.

Before trusting this on the real model, it was checked on a fake, built-to-order example where the right answer is known in advance: three independent made-up computations, mixed together into one shared set of numbers so that no single number gives away which computation it belongs to, with one of the three deliberately built from two related sub-parts. The method recovered the three groups exactly, correctly kept the two sub-parts together as a pair before merging them with the third group, and correctly identified the smallest set of pieces needed to reproduce each group's own behaviour. One thing it could not measure honestly: whether two grouped pieces "cooperate" (matter more together than separately) is impossible to tell from a simple straight-line reconstruction like this one — that check needs pieces to actually interact, which the method doesn't have access to. Good to know before reading anything into that number from the real run.

Run for real on the first MLP layer's 4608 pieces (top 300 by estimated importance, real Common Crawl text), the grouping is reproducible: splitting the same pieces on two independent halves of the data gives closely matching groups (a standard cluster-agreement score of 0.58, against 0.00 for a shuffled/meaningless control). Three groups stood out and were read by their real firing examples — not by asking "what output direction does this group's weight point toward," which turned out to be the wrong question and gave unreadable answers, but by literally reading the sentences where each group fires hardest and the sentences where it fires most negatively:

That reads as one group each for: which article to use, which end-of-sentence mark to use, and whether "I ___" continues as a full verb or a contraction. A matched-size random set of pieces reads far less cleanly on the same test (concentration 6.3 of 8 on average, against 7.7 of 8 for the three found groups) — the grouping is doing real work, not just picking out any large handful of pieces.

A caveat on how much of the layer this covers, worth stating plainly: those three named groups come from only the top 300 of the layer's 4608 pieces, ranked by a rough importance score — under 7%. It's tempting to assume the important pieces are the top ones and the rest is filler, but that assumption turns out to be wrong. Running the exact same grouping on the next 300 pieces, the ones just below the cutoff, they cluster every bit as cleanly (agreement score 0.56, versus 0.58 for the top group) and yield three more clean, nameable groups that weren't in the top set at all: one that fires before line breaks, one that fires before pronouns ("he", "they", "you"), and one that fires before spelled-out numbers ("six", "seven", "nine"). So the importance ranking doesn't actually separate the structured pieces from the unstructured ones — clean circuitry extends well past where we stopped looking. The honest read is that this layer holds at least six distinct nameable groups, probably more, and the survey so far has sampled a slice, not the whole. That's a limit on coverage, not on the method; the fix is simply to keep going down the list.

Running the same grouping at different depths tells a second, cleaner story about where in the network readable structure lives. It is not spread evenly, and it is not simply "front-loaded" — it is U-shaped. The first couple of layers cluster cleanly into the surface groups already described. A middle layer clusters far more weakly and barely reads as anything nameable — the middle of this network does abstract, tangled work that doesn't decompose into legible token-level pieces. But the final layer clusters more cleanly than any layer in the model, including the first — and into exactly what you'd expect right before the output: direct next-token readouts (two separate groups that fire when a line break is coming, one that fires before capitalized names). It even writes into a startlingly small space — about eight directions carry almost all of its output, versus hundreds in the middle. So the division of labor by depth is clean: the front builds features out of the raw tokens, the middle does the hard abstract work that resists this kind of reading, and the end reads out concrete next-token predictions. The two ends are the legible bookends of an illegible middle — the readout layer a near-mirror of the token-lookup table the very first layer turned out to be.

One caution comes with that picture, and it's a sharp one: legible is not the same as important. The readout layer's groups are the cleanest to read in the whole model, but when you switch them off they barely matter — that final layer moves the line-break decision about three times less than the first layer does, and its cleanest line-break group, deleted, does less than a random handful of its pieces. The clean readout groups are a tidy display of a decision that was largely made earlier and elsewhere, shown legibly right before the output because that's where each piece's activity is nearly a word prediction already. So the place a decision is easiest to read off is precisely not the place it is chiefly made. It's worth holding onto whenever a late-layer feature looks like the answer: reading a group and testing whether it matters are different questions, and here they point in opposite directions.

All three groups were then tested causally, not just read off examples — with a group's pieces replaced by their average value everywhere else in the layer left exact, does the model specifically lose the behaviour the examples suggested? This took three attempts to measure honestly, and the honest result is less clean than the reading-by-examples made it look. The first attempt used the model's overall next-word error rate, which was far too blunt an instrument — the effect was too small a slice of everything the model is doing at once to show up against the noise, on every comparison tried, including one that looked like a clean negative result before that was caught. Switching to a direct, targeted score — the model's preference for one word over another at each position, nothing else — did find real effects: every group's removal moves its targeted score 3 to 7 times more than removing a random same-size set of pieces does, every time, under three different ways of scoring "preference" (a raw score difference, an averaged version of it, and the model's actual probability). That part replicates cleanly. What does not replicate is which direction the effect points: for the "am"/"had"-vs-"'s" group, the raw-score version found it pushing the expected way on both sides, but the probability version found it pushing the opposite way on both sides — same group, same removal, opposite causal story depending only on which reasonable way of scoring "preference" is used. The "a"/"an"-vs-"the" group told two different one-sided stories depending on the same choice — confirmed on the "a"/"an" side with one scoring method, confirmed on the "the" side instead with the other. And the period/exclamation-vs-dash group's test turned out to be built on an unfair footing no matter how it was scored: dashes are simply a low- confidence guess for this model in general, correct context or not, so a clean two-way comparison against them doesn't work as designed. The honest summary at that point: the grouping finds real, specific structure — solid, reproduces on held-out data every time — but turning "these pieces move together" into "and here is exactly what causal job they do" had not yet worked for any of the three groups with a scoring test built on deleting the group and watching what breaks.

Deleting a group and watching the damage turned out to be the wrong shape of test, not a dead end. The standard alternative in this kind of work is to replace instead of delete — take the "a"/"an" group's real internal state from a real sentence that is about to say "a" or "an", and drop it into a different, real sentence that was about to say "the" instead, changing nothing else about that second sentence's own computation. If the group really carries that decision, the second sentence should tip toward "a"/"an" too. It does — four times more than swapping in a same-size random handful of pieces instead, and twenty-two times more than swapping in the same kind of state from another "the"-leaning sentence (so it's the borrowed content that matters, not just disturbing the sentence), with no comparable effect when the same swap is tried somewhere the a/an-versus-the choice doesn't even apply. Deleting this group left a murky picture; swapping its real content between two real sentences resolved it cleanly. The lesson survives even though the first three attempts at it didn't: components that look clean from the outside can have a genuinely tangled causal role — the fix was asking the right kind of question, not giving up on causal testing.

The same swap test was then run on the other two groups, closing the book on all three. The punctuation group stayed genuinely inconclusive, and this time for a diagnosed reason rather than a shrug: real sentences where a dash is the actually-correct next character turned out to be rare and noisy in this sample (about 50, against thousands for the article choice), so there wasn't enough clean signal for any test, swap included, to read. The "am"/"had"-vs-"'s" group told a different and more interesting story: the swap test finds a real, specific effect on that choice — but running backwards from what reading its own examples suggested, and this is now the third independent way of measuring it (two scoring-based attempts before this one) that agrees on the reversed direction. That's not a failed test; three separate methods landing on the same answer is about as solid as a causal claim gets in this report. The honest correction: this group doesn't promote the auxiliary reading the way its own top examples looked like it should — it pushes the opposite way, and exactly why is still open.

And it isn't a one-off. A different newly-found group — one that fires before line breaks — got the same swap test and came out the same way: a real, specific effect (well above what random pieces or same-context swaps produce), but pushing away from line breaks rather than toward them. So two of the groups tested now show this "reads one way, acts the other" split, while the article groups act exactly as they read. The lesson is worth stating plainly, because it's a caution about a whole style of interpretation: reading off the sentences where a group fires reliably tells you where real, specific structure lives — that part holds up every time — but it does not reliably tell you which direction that structure pushes the model. Which way a group actually steers the output is a separate question that has to be tested, and for a real fraction of groups the answer is the opposite of what the reading suggested. (A follow-up tried to pin the reversal down with a second kind of causal test; that test turned out too insensitive to settle it — it couldn't even recover the known-correct article result — so the reversal stands on the swap test, and its underlying cause is left open rather than overclaimed.)

One clean question cuts across all six of the first layer's named groups and sidesteps the direction puzzle entirely, because it's about what each group reads, not which way it pushes: does the group decide from the current word alone, or does it need the words before? The test from earlier — switch off the previous-word lookup and see whose firing collapses — applied to all six at once, lines them up on a clean spectrum, and the order is exactly what the grammar predicts:

That ordering isn't imposed, it falls out of the measurement — and it reads as sensible linguistics. You genuinely cannot tell whether "a" or "the" comes next from the word in front of you; you need the verb or preposition before it, so that group leans hardest on context. A spelled-out number, by contrast, is mostly recognizable from the local text right there, so that group barely uses context at all. The model isn't uniformly building "local" or "contextual" features in its first layer — it spends exactly as much context as each decision actually requires. (This particular result is clean regardless of the direction puzzle above, since it only asks what feeds each group, not which way the group steers.)

The same grouping method was then run on the transformer's second layer, to see whether it was a one-layer trick. It finds real structure there too — held up on a fair reproducibility check — but more weakly than the first layer, and murkier to read: one large group turned out to be detecting a byte-level text-encoding artifact (a replacement-character symbol from imperfectly-decoded curly quotes), not a linguistic behaviour; one looks like it might do related work to the first layer's article group; and the third reads only weakly. The method generalizes. Both what it finds and how confidently it can be causally pinned down get harder outside the one layer this thread started on.

The one open lead from that second-layer pass got the same swap test the first layer's groups did, and this time every check landed cleanly: swapping in a real "a"/"an"-leaning activation shifts the same article choice the right way, four to nine times more than the random-piece and same-leaning controls (the random control's own average even moved the wrong way, underlining how specific the real effect is), with nothing comparable where the choice doesn't apply. This is a genuinely different group of pieces, in the transformer's second layer, independently doing real, confirmed work on the same decision the first layer's article group makes — sized at about an eighth of the first layer's effect. Put together, that's the clearest answer this report has found to a question asked all the way back at the start of this line of work: does the model keep reusable machinery for the same job in more than one place? Here, yes — genuinely redundant, confirmed by the same standard of evidence in both layers, with one layer doing most of the work and the other contributing a real but minor share.

That one confirmed circuit — the article choice — was then traced end to end, which is worth more than adding another shallow layer to the survey. Two questions, both answered cleanly. First, what feeds it: the first MLP layer sits immediately after the first attention layer, so its input is the current word plus whatever that attention layer (an exact previous-word lookup) folds in. Switch off that attention layer and the article group's firing almost entirely collapses — it drops to barely a quarter of its real value — while switching off a later attention layer, which physically cannot reach this point, changes it not at all (a clean control confirming the test only registers real upstream influence). So the "a"/"an"-versus-"the" decision is not read off the current word; it is driven by the preceding context, exactly as it should be — you choose the article from the words before it. Second, where it goes: the second layer's redundant copy of this circuit doesn't just make the same decision, it fires on the same sentences — the two groups' activity across thousands of positions tracks together, while an unrelated group in the same second layer tracks not at all. The circuit now runs unbroken from the input: word → previous-word lookup → article group → the model's article prediction, with a matching second-layer echo firing in the same places — every link either exact arithmetic or a passed causal test. That is the shape of a circuit understood deeply rather than merely spotted.

One more step nails down the very front of that chain — which actual words trigger the decision. Sorting every real "a"/"an"/"the" position by what the article group is doing and by the word just before it, the pattern is mostly English grammar read straight off the circuit — with one correction a later causal test forced. corrected Forms of "to be" — "was ___", "is ___" — drive the group toward "a"/"an" (as in "is a doctor"), while a comma or period just before drives it toward "the." Prepositions drive it toward "the" as well — "of the", "in the", "to the" — in fact the strongest definite pull of any context; an earlier activation-only reading had lumped prepositions in with "to be" as indefinite-article triggers, but ablating the front attention and re-measuring shows the opposite direction. And the group's internal leaning genuinely predicts the real next word: positions it pushes toward "a"/"an" are markedly more likely to actually be "a"/"an" than the ones it pushes the other way. So the front of the chain isn't just "some earlier context" — it is nameable: after "is"/"was," reach for the indefinite article; after a preposition, a comma, or a period, reach for "the." A companion test traces the newline decision the same way: a sentence-ending "." , "!", or "?" just before lifts the chance of a line break from 1% to 30%, and it is the front attention, not the token-local MLP, that carries that jump — the MLP if anything pushes the other way. Two circuits, named from the triggering word to the output.

A last question about the two-layer copy: does the second layer's version read the first's answer and pass it along, or do the two work the problem independently from the same evidence? Removing the first layer's article group entirely and watching the second layer's copy settles it — the second copy barely flinches (about a one-percent change, actually less than what a random unrelated group in the first layer causes). It isn't listening to the first layer at all. Both copies read the same upstream evidence — the preceding words, via that previous-word lookup — and reach the same verdict on their own. So the redundancy is two independent witnesses, not a game of telephone: they fire on the same sentences because they read the same input, not because one hands off to the other. That is the more robust arrangement of the two — either copy alone still gets the answer — and it's a concrete, tested fact about how this model spreads one decision across layers.

Zooming all the way out to the whole 18-layer stack, one last measurement asks where the article decision actually lives: switch off each layer in turn and watch how much the a/an versus the choice moves. The profile is remarkably clean — the decision is built at the front (the first two layers do almost all of it), carried untouched through the entire middle (layers 3 through 14 barely move it at all — a fifteen-fold drop from the front), and then re-read once more at the very end, with the final layer the second biggest contributor of all. Front-build, empty middle, final readout. It matches the shape the rest of the report found for the middle of this model: a nearly-linear pipe that carries early decisions forward without redoing them. One surprise corrects an earlier emphasis: measured whole-layer, the second layer moves the article choice about eight times more than the first — the first layer is where the cleanest readable group happened to turn up, but the second layer quietly does more of the actual work. (Switching off a whole layer mixes its own direct effect with the knock-on disruption to everything downstream, so the exact sizes are upper bounds; but every layer gets the identical treatment, so the shape of the profile — and which layers matter — is a fair comparison.)

One more test forces an honest correction to the whole framing, and it's important enough to state plainly. Throughout this section a "group" of pieces has been treated as the place a decision is made. But there are two different questions hiding in that, and they turn out to have different answers. One: can you take a group's activity from one sentence, drop it into another, and steer the article choice? Yes — that's the swap test, and the article group passes it cleanly. Two: if you switch that same group off, does the decision break? Here the answer is no — switching off even the cleanest article group barely moves the article choice at all; it accounts for only a few percent of what switching off its whole layer does. Both answers are real and both are consistent, once you see what they mean together: the model computes the article choice redundantly, smeared across the entire width of the layer, so the readable group genuinely carries the signal (you can transplant it and it works) but is nowhere near necessary (you can delete it and the rest of the layer still gets the answer). And this isn't a quirk of one group — it holds for the flagship, in both layers that do the work, so it's the general shape of the thing. The honest headline, then, is not "we localized the article circuit to these pieces." It is: the grouping finds real, readable, sufficient pieces of a decision — a genuine handle on it, with legible triggers and a traceable path — but the decision itself lives redundantly across the whole layer, with no small necessary core in the list of pieces. That is a sharper and more useful thing to know than a false claim of localization, and it's the kind of correction this whole effort is built to surface rather than paper over. (The same test on a second, unrelated decision — where line breaks go — gives the same answer, so this is how the model works, not a quirk of articles.)

But "no necessary core in the list of pieces" is not the end of the story, because a piece is only one way to slice a layer. A layer's output is a bundle of 1152 numbers, and you can ask the necessity question of combinations of those numbers instead of the individual pieces that wrote them. Do that — keep only a handful of the most important output directions and blur the rest — and the picture flips: just sixteen directions, out of 1152, carry about 84% of the whole layer's effect on the article choice, and those particular sixteen do six times better than sixteen random ones. So the decision does have a compact necessary core — it was simply hiding in the wrong coordinate system. Spread across every piece, yes; but those pieces are all pushing within one small sixteen-dimensional subspace. Both facts are true at once, and together they're the honest shape of the thing: the computation is distributed across all the pieces that do it, and compact in the handful of directions they collectively write into — the same "many quiet contributors, one small shared channel" pattern this report keeps finding elsewhere in the model. The right thing to call "the circuit," then, isn't a list of pieces at all; it's that small output subspace. The line-break decision has the same two-sided shape — spread across the pieces, compact in the directions — just a bit looser: it takes about sixty-four directions rather than sixteen to capture it, and no single one dominates. So the compactness is a dial, not a fixed number: tighter for the article choice, looser for line breaks, but low-rank either way.

A companion tool from the same outside source does the compression side of this question properly: given a layer's weight and its real activations, it computes — in one exact step, no guessing or gradient descent — the best possible low-rank stand-in for that layer on the data it actually sees. First checked against a number this report already trusts: a different measurement, elsewhere in this program, found the first attention layer needs only 16 of its 1152 directions to keep its cost under a fixed bar. Building the naive version of this cross-check — combining the layer's two internal weight matrices into one and testing that — gave a nonsense answer (a supposedly free substitution that cost nearly 5 nats), traced immediately to a real modeling mistake: the layer has 9 independent attention heads sharing one output matrix, and you cannot combine two matrices that don't share a single consistent pattern across positions. Redone the way that already worked earlier in this thread — feed the tool the real activations flowing into the one genuinely single matrix in the pipeline, not an invented combination — the new tool's answer landed almost exactly on the number already trusted: its own rank-16 stand-in costs 0.094, just under the same bar, and beats a same-size random direction by more than fourteen times. Two independent measurements, two different methods, the same answer.

With the tool trusted, the natural next question was whether the first attention layer's real behaviour splits into different cases the way the first MLP's did — recall the earlier grouping work found MLP pieces that specialize (an article choice here, a punctuation choice there). Run the same recursive-splitting search on attention with the newly-confirmed rank-16 anchor in hand this time (rather than guessing, as the very first MLP attempt had to), and the search finds nothing to split — one leaf, the whole dataset, never refined. That is a real, informative difference, not a failure of the search: the first MLP layer's version of this same search found five distinct groups, some needing far fewer directions than the rest. Put the two results together and a clean picture emerges — the first attention layer really is what earlier sections called it, a single uniform lookup table that treats the whole vocabulary the same way and needs the same ~16 numbers everywhere, while the first MLP layer is a genuinely mixed bag, with most of its work generic and a real minority of special cases that compress much further. Two components, two honest answers, from the same tool pointed at each in turn.

Reading a feature and steering it are different directions

The last MLP layer carries a clean signal for what comes next. Fit the direction in its output that best separates "the next token is a newline" from everything else, and that direction flags newline positions correctly 84% of the time on held-out text — a real decoder. The obvious next move is to use it as a lever: add it to the model's final internal state and the model should predict more newlines. It does the opposite. Pushing along the newline decoder drives the newline probability steadily down, from 2.5% to 1.4% as the push grows; pushing the other way raises it. The article decoder behaves identically — pushing it drops the article probability from 8% to 3%. A direction validated as a detector fails, and reverses, as a control. corrected The supervised readout directions are decoders, not intervention handles.

The reason is not that the decoder points the wrong way — it points nowhere near the lever. The direction that actually controls the newline prediction is the newline row of the output embedding, the vector the model scores the final state against. The angle between the decoder and that control direction is essentially ninety degrees: their cosine is 0.00, for both newline and article. They are unrelated. Push the control direction instead and the feature is completely under your thumb — adding the newline output-embedding row takes newline probability from 2.5% to 93%, and the article row takes article probability from 6% to 98%; subtract either and the probability goes to zero. The decoder cannot steer because it is not on the steering axis at all; its effect on the prediction is no bigger than a random direction's. So every feature has two directions: one you read it from, fit on the layer's activity, and one you write it with, the output-embedding row — and here they are orthogonal. This is the same fact as an earlier puzzle in this program, that the cleanest place to read a token-class signal (the last layer) is not where the model decides it (earlier layers): reading and deciding use different directions. The split is sharper than location alone. Delete the readout layer's whole contribution and the newline probability doesn't fall, it rises — from 43% to 65% at the positions where newlines belong, and article from 39% to 54%. The layer where these classes are easiest to read is quietly suppressing them, and the suppression is a frequency calibration: across the whole vocabulary, the more common a token is the harder the last layer pushes it down (correlation 0.64 between a token's frequency and how much deleting the layer lifts it), and the twelve most frequent continuations — the commas, periods, newlines, and function words — are exactly the ones it holds back most. It is a trade the model wins: removing the last layer makes common targets slightly easier (−0.17 nats) but rare targets much harder (+0.69 nats), for a net loss of 0.43 nats — the layer spends a little accuracy on frequent words to buy more on rare ones. That correction turns out to be one specific direction: a single rank-1 axis carries it, removing that axis destroys the calibration while removing a random direction does nothing, and the axis is ~60% aligned with the output head's own frequency direction. The model actually applies this frequency correction twice, in two separate places — a mid-network representation-level pass (blocks 4–6) and the readout-level one here (blocks 16–17) — with the last block doing the bulk. The actual writing happens early: removing the second block costs the most, and the first three blocks together do the bulk of it. To detect a feature, fit a probe; to change it, push the output-embedding row; and don't expect the layer that reads it best to be the one that writes it.

What each part of the network does

Deleting one block at a time and watching where the prediction breaks turns the eighteen blocks into three jobs with clean boundaries. Ask each block, for nine kinds of next token — newlines, determiners, prepositions, digits, punctuation, capitalized words, ordinary words — how much removing it lowers the probability of that kind of token when it is the right answer. For every one of the nine, the biggest writer is in the first three blocks: the class of the next token — is it a word, a number, a line break — is decided at the very front, and the rest of the network never overrides that choice. Removing the front three blocks costs seven nats of loss; removing any three-block slice of the middle costs well under one.

So what is the middle for? Not nothing — but not the class. Delete the middle eleven blocks and the probability of the exactly-correct next token collapses by 93%, while the probability of the correct class falls by only 55%; delete the front three and both fall together. The middle keeps the class the front chose and spends its effort picking which member of that class — which specific word — and it spends that effort disproportionately on rare words, exactly the ones a single guess is least likely to get right. The front decides the kind of token; the middle refines it to the token. And the last block, as the previous section showed, does neither — it recalibrates, trimming the common words the front over-predicts to make room for the rare ones the middle is trying to name. Three stages, three jobs: decide the class, choose the word, fix the frequencies. One caveat on the arithmetic: deleting an early block also starves every block after it, so the front's seven-nat cost is inflated by that knock-on and is not a clean measure of the front's own share; the durable facts are that the middle's own cost is small and falls on rare words, and that it protects the class while sacrificing the word.

Where the circuits bottom out: a bigram the network sharpens

Follow the article and newline circuits all the way down and they end at the simplest possible place — the embedding table. Skip all eighteen blocks and read the current token's embedding straight through the output head, and the triggers are already there: a period predicts a line break 42% of the time — about right, matching how often a period really does end a line — while "of"/"in" reach for "the" and "is"/"was" reach for "a"/"an." The relative pull is memorized in the embedding geometry: a period's embedding leans toward a line break more than an ordinary word's does. But it is only a handful of directions, not a working model — read every prediction this way and the shortcut scores worse than guessing uniformly, because the embedding and output head were trained to work through the eighteen blocks, not as a direct map. The trigger preferences are in the embedding; turning them into an actual predictor is the network's job.

So what are the eighteen blocks for, on these predictions? Not to build the trigger — to discriminate it. The embedding's bigram is context-blind: it fires the same after every period. Split the periods by what actually comes next and the bigram cannot tell them apart — it predicts a line break equally (0.42 vs 0.42) whether a line break really follows or not. The full network separates them cleanly: it lifts the chance to 0.47 at the periods that truly end a line and cuts it to 0.21 at the ones in the middle of a paragraph. The blocks read the surrounding context to decide which of the bigram's blanket guesses are right. The same period fans out three ways — a line break, a capitalized new sentence, or a lowercase continuation — and the network routes cleanly among them: where a new sentence actually follows it swaps its bet, dropping the line-break chance to 0.22 and lifting the capital-letter chance to 0.45, a flip the context-blind bigram (which fires the same 0.42/0.10 at every period) never makes. A few classes are different — digits, punctuation, capitalized words are barely in the embedding bigram (they start at a few percent) and the network builds them almost from scratch, four to eight times up. But for the function-word triggers the story is uniform: the embedding proposes, the network disposes, and the last layer trims the frequencies. Trigger, discrimination, calibration — three jobs, and only the middle one needs depth.

One knob you can turn, and a machine you can only read

Step back and the interventions sort into two piles with almost nothing in between. Ask "is there a single direction I can delete to switch this behaviour off?" and the answer is yes for exactly one thing: the last layer's frequency calibration is a single rank-1 direction — remove it and the calibration is gone, remove a random direction of the same size and nothing happens. That one knob is worth about 0.43 of the 7.5 nats the network adds over blind guessing — call it six percent of the model's skill.

Everything else — the routing that decides whether a period ends a line, whether an article is coming, which content word to emit, and even the model's memory of whether it is inside a quote or an open parenthesis — has no such knob. These are all readable: a linear probe recovers "inside a parenthesis" at 92% accuracy, and the model acts on it hard (a close-paren is six hundred times likelier inside an open one). But delete that readable direction and the behaviour is untouched — the state is decoded from a place that isn't where it is caused. Reading a feature out and being able to switch it off are different powers, and the model grants the first far more freely than the second. So the honest shape of this network is one clean linear knob bolted onto a large, redundant, distributed machine: legible in patches, editable almost nowhere.

Two things this architecture does without softmax

bilin18 has no softmax anywhere — not in attention, not at the output — and two of its quirks turn out to be how it copes. The first is in the residual stream: a handful of dimensions grow to twenty, forty, sixty times the size of the rest by the final block, and they carry about 85% of the stream's total energy. These "massive activations" are a known curiosity in large models, usually pinned to attention sinks — but here they are not sinks (they fire the same on every token and position, because there is no softmax to dump attention onto a special one). Instead they are the model's volume knob. The output is normalised by the size of the whole vector, and these few huge dimensions set that size; subtract their constant part and the normalisation is wrong, and the loss jumps 1.6 nats — more than removing their varying signal does. The model grows a few enormous dimensions on purpose, to fix the gain of its own readout.

The second is in attention itself. A normal transformer scores each key once and sharpens the winners with an exponential; bilin18 has no exponential, so a single score would attend half the context — barely better than random. It sharpens a different way: every head computes two independent scores per key and multiplies them. Each score alone is diffuse (it lands on ~54% of keys), but the product is focal (~23%) — because it is large only where both scores are large. And the two scores are uncorrelated (correlation near zero across 120 of the 162 heads), so this is a genuine two-part test: a key is attended only if it passes two different criteria at once. Where softmax attention asks one question of each key, this architecture asks two and takes the AND — a more expressive primitive, bought with a second query-key projection per head instead of a normalisation.

And it is the same trick twice. The MLP does exactly what the attention does: each hidden unit multiplies two linear projections of its input, each of which fires on ~62% of tokens on its own, but whose product is selective (32%) — sparse for the same reason the attention is focal, large only where both factors are. So the whole network, attention and MLP alike, runs on one primitive: multiply two linear maps and let the product be the gate. That single multiplicative AND stands in for both of a normal transformer's nonlinearities — the softmax in attention and the ReLU in the MLP — at once. "Bilinear" is not a detail of one layer; it is the entire computational style of the model.

The current token never leaves the stream

Every block rescales the residual as x = λ₀·x + λ₁·x₀, where x₀ is the original token embedding. Reading those learned weights off the model shows a deliberate pattern: λ₁ ≈ 8 at almost every one of the 18 blocks — the embedding is re-added, at eight times strength, over and over — while λ₀ resets the running residual near the front (block 1's λ₀ is 0.013, block 5's 0.064, each almost erasing what came before) and only accumulates normally in the back half. A write made in block 0 arrives at the output scaled by about 2×10⁻⁴; a write made late arrives intact.

The consequence is measurable at the readout. In a normal transformer the current token is gradually transformed into contextual features and is no longer recoverable at the top. Here it is: a linear probe recovers the current token's identity (via its log-frequency) from the residual at R² 0.91 at the embedding, 0.85 at block 8, and still 0.73 at the final block — a slow fade, not a transformation-away (a shuffled-label control sits at −0.43). The model keeps the current token linearly present all the way to the output, and the blocks add their contextual computation on top of an ever-present embedding rather than replacing it. This is why the embedding "triggers" that seed every circuit stay available at the readout: they are re-injected at every layer.

One tidy negative closes the loop between this and the volume-knob dimensions above. If the embedding is re-added eight times, do its largest dimensions become the massive ones? No. The embedding is dimensionally flat — its biggest dimension is only 1.5× its median — while the final residual is peaked 58×, and the two sets of top dimensions barely overlap (2 of 10, rank correlation 0.14). The persistent embedding and the gain-setting massive dimensions are two independent mechanisms sharing one residual stream: one keeps the token present (spread across many dimensions), the other is built by the blocks to fix the readout's gain.

Building the layers up, bottom to top

The class code is multi-axis. The "class" the front computes is not one of a few buckets but a point in a multi-attribute space: capitalization, determiner-ness, punctuation, number, space-prefix and more are each separately decodable and sit on near-orthogonal axes (capitalization vs determiner overlap just 0.10). So a token can be several categories at once — "The" scores high on both the determiner axis and the capital axis, where "the" is determiner-only and "London" capital-only. The eight labels used elsewhere here are a coarse single-label readout of this richer ~24-dimensional code.

Reading each early layer as a transformation of the ones below it — by its actual bilinear weights and by how it re-clusters the token geometry, rather than by a coarse label — gives a concrete, mechanism-level account of the front, and a caution against calling any layer "redundant" too quickly.

Layer 0. The MLP computes grammatical class as a bank of bilinear self-product detectors: each class-writing unit is essentially (readout·x)² where the readout selects one category, so the square sharpens that category (~1.5×). Reading the units by their top tokens gives clean detectors — determiner, capitalized/proper, punctuation, conjunction, number, content-word. In parallel, the layer-0 attention writes the previous token into the stream (the previous token decodes from its output at 0.86 versus 0.30 at its input) — a copy-source. So after layer 0 the stream holds the current token, its sharpened grammatical class, and the previous token.

Layer 1 is not a repeat of layer 0 — an early guess that it was turned out to be an artifact of using a coarse 8-way label, which cannot see the fine geometry. A token-conditional mean is a clustering, and the right question is how the relative token-to-token similarities move from stage to stage. They move a lot: layer 0 changes the geometry (similarity-structure correlation with the embedding drops to 0.49) and collapses the effective dimension from 117 to 20 — a hard clustering by class; then layer 1 changes it almost as much (0.58) and does the geometric opposite, re-expanding to 47 dimensions. Folding layer 1's readouts onto the features layer 0 computed shows what it re-reads to do that: class most of all, then position, and — modestly — the previous token and finer token identity. So layer 1 re-inflates the class-collapsed representation by folding position and fine token structure back in.

Attention at layer 5 is the biggest early component and resisted every grammatical probe — it carries neither token identity nor class nor context-class better than a plain head. Its role only showed up through its effect: ablating it hurts content-word prediction far more than function words (a mean +2.7 nats on content words, 62% of its total effect, versus ~1.0 on determiners and punctuation). So layer 5's attention is an early content contributor — it works on the hard lexical part of the loss, which is exactly why a grammatical lens was blind to it.

And we can now say more about layer 5: it is the early content head and the induction gate. Feed the model a random token sequence repeated twice and it predicts the second copy almost perfectly — the loss falls from 13.1 nats on the first pass to 1.3 on the second, pure copying of what-followed-this-token -last-time (random tokens have no other structure to exploit). Ablate attention one layer at a time and layer 5 is the gate: removing it wipes out essentially all of that induction, with layers 0–2 each also required (a front-to-back chain — the previous-token head at layer 0 lays down the match key, layer 5 executes the copy). On natural text the same ablation collapses the copy discount — a token whose bigram has occurred before costs 0.7 nats against 3.5 for a new one, and removing layer 5 erases that gap — while a control middle layer does nothing. But layer 5 is not only an induction head: removing it also hurts first mentions (which have nothing to copy), so it does general content aggregation too, of which induction is one job. That resolves the old puzzle of layer 5's unexplained "content" help — part of it is literally copying content words it has already seen. The copying is graded, not all-or-nothing: even words that recur without an exact repeated bigram get steadily cheaper the more recently and more often they have appeared (from 2.5 nats for a distant single prior mention down to 1.3 for a recent, frequent one), and layer 5 carries the long-range end of that — ablating it costs most where the previous mention is far back.

The whole stack, as geometry. Tracking the token-similarity structure through all eighteen layers gives a clean shape: two expansions and three collapses. Effective dimension goes 117 → 20 (layer 0 collapses to class) → 47 (layer 1 re-expands) → ~45 → 23 (layer 5) → 51 at layer 9, the peak, where the middle re-inflates the representation → then a long decline → 6.5 → 2.8 at the last two layers, where the readout compresses most of the residual's variance into a roughly three-to-four-dimensional subspace. That collapse is about variance, not prediction: those few loud directions carry the grammar/boundary structure, but the actual next-token prediction is high-rank — even the top 256 variance-directions leave a chunk of the loss on the table, and the content prediction lives in the quiet, high-rank tail beneath the loud grammatical axes. The relative-similarity structure is reorganized mainly at the front (layers 0–1) and again at the readout (layer 15→16); through the middle it is nearly frozen. So the middle is dimensionally active — it re-inflates — while barely changing the clustering topology, and the readout does the final, sharpest re-clustering down to the prediction.

What the middle does — attention aggregates topic, MLPs read it. The one gap left was what the middle computes, and two experiments closed it. Decode a token's topic (the §-content clusters) from the residual at each layer: the accuracy climbs steadily with depth — 0.37 near the front, 0.63 by layer 5, 0.82 by layer 12, 0.85 near the readout, against a 0.20 chance floor and a 0.18 shuffled-label control. Split each layer's gain into its attention part and its MLP part, and the rise is almost entirely attention: every layer's attention step raises topic decodability (+0.20 summed across the middle) while the MLP steps add essentially nothing (−0.02). Yet folding each MLP's bilinear readouts onto the topic directions shows the MLPs read topic strongly at every depth. So the division of labor is clean: attention is the topic aggregator — it pulls the surrounding context's subject matter into the residual, a little more at each layer — and the MLPs are topic readers that consume that structure to push toward specific words, without themselves making topic more decodable. That is the whole content chain: attention aggregates context → a topic representation builds up across the middle → the MLPs read it → the readout emits topically-coherent words.

Two machines. Pulling the whole trace together, the network separates into two systems that happen to share the same layers. A grammar machine turns the current token into a sharpened part-of-speech and a coarse position — the front MLPs do this from the token alone, almost without context (delete every attention head and the grammar loss barely moves), and it is low-rank, nameable, and steerable; it is also the easy quarter of the loss. A content machine chooses the specific next word: attention aggregates the surrounding context and the MLPs — mostly the last few — convert it into a prediction. Deleting attention hurts word-choice four times as much as grammar, so the content machine runs on context the way the grammar machine runs on the current token. This half is built gradually across the depth, concentrated at the back, and it is diffuse — no low-rank handle, no clean names — and it is three-quarters of the loss. The clean, interpretable machinery and the bulk of the difficulty are not the same machine: grammar is small, structured, and cheap; content is large, distributed, and most of the work. And the two really are separate machines, not just a convenient description: although attention builds both of them, on largely the same heads, it writes them into orthogonal channels of the residual — the grammar and topic subspaces overlap at chance, and swapping a passage's topic barely touches its predicted part-of-speech (about 7% of the effect of editing grammar directly). So the model runs one shared context engine feeding two independently-editable subspaces. And this two-channel organisation is not peculiar to bilin18: the grammar and content subspaces are near-orthogonal in GPT-2 and GPT-2-large too (overlap within about twice chance), so keeping grammar and content on separate channels appears to be a general property of language models, alongside the shared loss budget. And the two channels recombine at the readout to pick a single word — asymmetrically. The grammar channel is a dominant, low-rank gate that sets the word's part of speech: remove it and the predicted word's category collapses (and the loss blows up everywhere). The content channel is a diffuse, high-rank narrowing that shapes the specific new word, and its effect concentrates exactly where a new word is needed — removing it raises the word-choice loss most on first mentions, least on copied repeats — but no small subspace carries much of it. So the emitted word is grammar-gates-the-category intersected with content-picks-the-word, one strong and low-rank, the other weak-per-direction and high-rank.

A worked example makes it concrete. In the context "…pumping you up, and turning", the model predicts a pronoun — you, your, it — and its content channel has read the passage's topic as physiology (its nearest topical words are blood, lactate, patient). Delete the class channel at the readout and the prediction flips to punctuation — the model no longer knows it should emit a pronoun. Delete the content channel instead and it still predicts you/your: the part of speech is untouched, only the specific word would drift. That single case shows both machines at work — the grammar gate deciding what kind of word, the content narrowing which one — and shows their independence, since removing one leaves the other's job intact.

And the content machine is not nameless after all — it just needed the right question. Every probe that imposed a category (grammatical class, embedding-semantic clusters) came up empty, because the content is not organized that way. But strip the grammar out of the late residual and let the leftover cluster itself, then read each cluster by the words over-represented in it rather than its most frequent words, and clean topics fall out: one cluster is education (school, students, grades), another Orthodox religion (monastery, Holy, Fr, Seraph), others are technology and business (Apple, NYSE, HTC), Middle-East geopolitics, American football, vaccines, aviation, emergency medicine, prisons. The content machine is a topic tracker: it sorts the context by subject matter and pushes toward topically-coherent words. That also explains why it looked structureless — topic space is huge (many subjects, each with many words), so it has no small set of directions to find, but it is genuinely structured, not noise. So the honest end-state is not "grammar is interpretable, content is a wall" but "grammar is a low-rank part-of-speech computation; content is a high-dimensional topic computation" — both real, one just far larger than the other. A caution on the clusters, though: the topic space is continuous, so the twelve buckets are a way of drawing that continuum, not twelve real categories. Cluster the two halves of the data separately and the discrete labels don't line up — as any arbitrary slicing of a continuum wouldn't — but the continuous geometry underneath does: the dominant content directions in one half match the other at 25× chance. The topic structure is real and reproducible; the twelve names are just a readable slice of it.

Is the topic structure causal, or only a readable correlate? Steering the late residual along a topic's own direction does specifically raise that topic's distinctive next-words (mean own-topic gain +0.042, off-topic −0.004, consistent across every topic), so there is a real causal signal — but it is weak, and an honest control shows why we should not over-read it. Do the identical thing with the grammatical class instead of the topic and, at matched strength, the effect is about twice as large (own-class gain ~0.08 versus ~0.04) but still small. So the class is modestly more steerable than the topic — consistent with grammar being lower-rank, one direction capturing more of it — yet neither is steered strongly by a single direction. That is a general fact: pushing a feature's read direction barely steers, because the read and write directions are close to orthogonal in these models. So single-direction steering is a weak test for both machines, and the case that topic is causal rests better on the interventions that don't depend on it: the content-word gist survives function-word masking and word-order shuffling (a causal edit of the input), the first-mention floor, and the replicable geometry. And there is a proper causal test that does work: an interchange intervention. Instead of pushing a lone direction, take two passages on different subjects, and swap just the topic-subspace coordinates of one into the other at a late layer. The prediction moves toward the donor's subject — its topical words gain about 0.7 nats over the untouched case, far more than swapping a random subspace does. The same works for the grammatical class (swapping its subspace flips the predicted part-of-speech four times more often than a random swap). So both variables are genuinely causal; single-direction steering was weak only because it pushes the read direction, while interchange swaps the real stored activation and respects read and write together. The two machines are distinguished by their structure — grammar low-rank, local, context-free; content high-dimensional, long-range, topical — not by a difference in whether they are causal.

And we can say what the aggregator actually computes. Feed the model shorter and shorter slices of context: topic decodability climbs steadily with length and is still rising at the model's full 256-token window, and the word-choice loss falls four times as much as the grammar loss does — content lives on far context, grammar on the last few tokens. Then edit the context and re-read the topic: blank out the function words and 82% of the topic survives; blank out the content words instead and it collapses to near chance; shuffle the word order and 61% still survives even as the grammar loss jumps. So the topic is a content-word, order-insensitive gist — the model pools the topical words across the whole passage, largely regardless of their arrangement, which is exactly the shape a high-dimensional subject-matter summary should have. Grammar is the mirror image: it needs the current token and the local order, and every context edit that leaves topic intact damages it.

Where every nat goes. All of this lands in one budget. The model takes the average next-token cost from 7.17 nats (guessing from word frequencies alone) down to 3.24, split as 0.76 grammar + 2.48 content. Sort the tokens by what kind of prediction they are and the loss concentrates sharply. A token that just copies a repeated bigram (induction) costs 0.69 nats and there are few of them — 6% of tokens, 1% of the loss. A token whose type was seen but not as a repeat costs 1.94 — a third of tokens, a fifth of the loss. And a genuinely new word — a first mention — costs 4.24 nats, and these are 60% of tokens carrying 78% of the entire loss, almost all of it content rather than grammar. Grammar is a flat, cheap tax paid everywhere (0.76–0.84 in every group); content is what scales with novelty (0.39 when copyable, 1.10 when seen, 3.48 when new). So the model's real job, by the numbers, is the first-mention content problem — the topic-constrained but open choice of a new word — and that is exactly the part that survives even a much larger model. And this budget is not peculiar to bilin18: run the identical split on GPT-2 and GPT-2-large and first mentions carry 77% and 78% of the loss — against 78% here — with induction cheap and grammar flat in all three. The absolute cost of a new word does fall with scale (3.8 nats in GPT-2, 3.5 here, 3.35 in GPT-2-large), but where the loss sits does not move. The shape of the problem is a property of language modelling, not of this model. Nor is it a property of the bilinear MLP: a sister model of the same size trained on the same data with an ordinary SwiGLU MLP gives an identical budget (first mentions 78.4%, seen-other 20.3%, induction 1.3%), the same grammar/content split, and the same strong front-of-model induction — so the two-machine-plus-induction structure is in the learned computation, not the architecture we happened to read. A smaller sibling (12 layers, 162M) tells us which parts are scale-free and which are not: it has the very same budget (78% first mentions) and the same grammar/content split, but a much weaker general-purpose copy — it can still predict real repeated phrases cheaply, yet fails the pure random-token copy test that the larger models pass easily. So the structure of the loss is invariant to size and architecture; the strength of the induction circuit is what grows with scale. Changing the attention itself — a model with an ordinary normalized squared-attention instead of the two-branch bilinear kind — leaves the budget identical too, and in fact copies better at the same size, so induction strength depends on the attention design as well as scale, while the loss structure stays put across all of it.

Does the model know when it is guessing? Mostly. Its predictive entropy — how uncertain it says it is — is well-calibrated on average (3.25 nats of entropy against 3.26 of actual loss) and tracks the situation: it is confident on a copyable repeat (1.6 nats), unsure on a genuinely new word (3.7), with seen-again words in between, and its per-token entropy correlates with its per-token loss at 0.58. So the budget's structure is reflected in the model's own confidence — it represents which kind of prediction it is making. But the calibration is uneven in a telling way: it is over-cautious on the easy tokens (hedging more than it needs to on repeats) and over-confident on first mentions — most peaked exactly where it should be least sure. The "this is a new word" situation is weakly decodable from the residual throughout the stack (a little above the 60% base rate), so the signal is computed internally; the model just doesn't fully act on it when it commits to a new-word guess. This is not a quirk of this model: GPT-2 and GPT-2-large show the very same pattern — over-cautious on repeats, over-confident on first mentions — so the confidence structure, like the loss budget itself, is a general property of language models.

Reading the variables, not the heads

The honest unit of this model is not a head — heads are broad and multi-function — but the variable a computation reads and writes: a direction (or low-rank subspace) in the residual stream. Two questions decide whether we have really found one: can we identify it (ideally from the weights, since in a bilinear network weights and activations are the same kind of object), and can we intervene on it and change the output in the way an abstract account predicts.

Interchange beats steering. Pushing a feature's mean direction barely moved the output — which had made the causal status of both machines look weak. But that was the wrong test: it pushes the read direction, and read and write are near-orthogonal here. The right test is an interchange intervention — swap the variable's real activation subspace between two runs. Do that and both variables are clearly causal: swapping the grammatical-class subspace flips the predicted part-of-speech about four times more often than swapping a random subspace, and swapping the topic subspace between two passages moves the prediction toward the donor's subject by ~0.7 nats over a random swap. So the class and topic variables are real causal handles; steering was just the wrong lever.

Variables are continuously re-written through the stack. Which weights identify a variable depends on where you look, and tracing it layer by layer (rather than jumping from layer 0 to layer 15) shows why: the class variable is not transported, it is re-written at every layer. Each layer adds fresh class in its own directions; the running subspace is about 85% carried from the layer below plus a fresh write on top. Its alignment with the layer-0 write weights decays steadily (0.88 → 0.08), and its alignment with the readout's read weights rises (0.07 → 0.18), the freshly-written class crossing from write-aligned to read-aligned around layer 11 — a gradual drift across the whole stack, fastest at the front and the readout, not a single rotation point. And at no layer does either raw weight basis match the variable strongly (the write weights only at layer 0, the read weights peaking at 0.18): the variable lives in a maintained subspace of the activations that the weights only partially name. So the front-write weights fail to identify it at layer 15 because by then it has been re-written a dozen times; the honest handle is each layer's own activation subspace (a learned/DAS subspace does no better than the plain class-conditional-mean directions, so those are the variable).

How much of each layer is a variable we can write down. Replace a component with a lookup table keyed on our named variables and see how much of the loss it keeps, on a scale from mean-ablation (0) to the full model (1). The front is almost fully a token table — layer-0 MLP scores 0.94, a clean token→class-cluster map. The middle content MLPs score only ~0.4 on a token table, and the gap is filled mostly in-sample by two named variables — the previous token and the topic — but these two behave very differently once you test on held-out data. The topic, treated as the continuous subspace it really is rather than a dozen discrete buckets, genuinely generalizes (it is low-rank). The previous-token table does not: it looks like it fills much of the middle in-sample, but that is a giant bigram table memorizing the evaluation rows — on fresh text it adds nothing. So the middle's honestly-explained part is current token plus continuous topic; the local-context "fill" was overfitting. The readout, measured the same careful way, is largely token-determined. In short: the grammar machine is a variable we can write down; the content machine is a variable we can causally move but not yet tabulate.

Put it all together and replace every component at once with its named-variable stand-in, and the honest number is more sobering than the per-layer picture suggests — because it must be measured on held-out data. In-sample, the stand-ins recover 81% of the model; but that is mostly memorization. On fresh text the reconstruction is about 29%: current token recovers ~19%, the continuous topic adds a further ~10% that does generalize (it is low-rank), and the previous-token table — which looked like it filled much of the middle in-sample — adds essentially nothing out-of-sample. That bigram table was memorizing, not explaining. So the part of the model we can capture as a generalizing, named, low-rank variable is roughly a quarter to a third — the grammar skeleton plus a continuous topic direction — and the large remainder is real computation that we can move causally (the interchange swaps work) and describe as a mechanism (a content-word gist) but cannot yet write down as a compact, generalizing table. The gap between 81% in-sample and held-out is itself the lesson: understanding has to be certified on data the explanation was not fit to.

The final number depends on giving the variables their fair form. A discrete lookup table reconstructs about 30% of the model held-out — but a lookup fails on any token it didn't see in training, which penalizes even the front, where the computation really is a clean function of the token. Fit instead a smooth low-rank map of the same named variables (token embedding, previous-token embedding, continuous topic), and the honest held-out number rises to about 40%. It splits sharply by depth: the front grammar is recovered at ~0.90 — essentially solved, a smooth generalizing function of the current token — the readout at ~0.56, and the middle content at ~0.10 with a coarse topic — but that last number understates it, and chasing it down pins the frontier exactly. Give the middle content its fair form — a higher-rank continuous content feature read from the layer's own input — and it climbs to ~0.30 at rank 128 and ~0.44 at near-full rank; but it stops there, because the middle layers are bilinear (they multiply two projections of their input), so roughly half of what they compute is a genuine multiplicative nonlinearity that no linear read of the input can capture. So the middle is not a wall and not noise: it is a high-rank, half-nonlinear content computation — its output is ~0.44 a linear read of content plus ~0.56 a multiplicative interaction. That is the honest frontier: grammar is solved as a low-rank, writable, causal variable; content is causally real (the interchange swaps move it) and mechanistically a bilinear content-mixing computation, but not reducible to a table or a linear read — the remaining understanding gap is that nonlinearity.

How the layers divide the work — a barbell

Decomposing each MLP's output map on real data and asking how many directions it takes to reproduce what the layer does for the loss gives a clean depth profile. The two ends do the most work and are the simplest: the first MLP does 2.3 nats of work through 8 directions, and the last few (blocks 16–17) do ~1.6 nats through one to four directions each — block 16's entire contribution is a single direction. The early-middle layers (1–5) are genuinely high-rank (128–256 directions), and the deep middle (blocks 6–14) is nearly inert, ~0.03 nats apiece. The MLP work is a barbell: concentrated, low-rank, and legible at the edges; diffuse in the early-middle; absent in the deep middle.

A correction to that last clause. "Nearly inert" is what each middle layer looks like when you ablate it alone — and that reading is a trap. Remove the whole middle (layers 6–11) at once and the model loses 1.9 nats, nearly four times the sum of the individual ablations. The middle is not idle; its work is redundant — any one layer can go because the others cover for it, so single-layer ablation drastically understates the band. And that work is not something new: forcing the entire middle to keep only its class-and-position content recovers 65% of those 1.9 nats (a random subspace of the same size makes the model far worse than deleting the middle outright). So the middle's work is class-and-position too. A subtlety sharpens what it is doing: because the residual stream is additive, the front's output is still present when the middle is deleted — so the middle's cost means it is adding class-and-position content, not merely holding the front's in place. And that content is mostly in new directions: the middle's output is recovered by its own class-and-position directions (65%) far better than by the front's (37%). So the middle keeps computing class and position — roughly half reinforcing the front's variables, half adding fresh structure — rather than passively maintaining them. The whole stack reads as one amortized pipeline: the front computes class and position, the middle keeps computing more of them, and the back reads them out into predictions.

And this three-part pipeline is not peculiar to bilin18. Running the same band analysis on five other models — GPT-2 small, medium, and large, and Pythia-160M and 410M — reproduces its defining feature in every one: a middle band that is redundant in exactly the same way, collectively worth 1.3 to 3.9 times the sum of its parts, and 59–78% class-and-position when kept in isolation. Six models across three architectures (bilinear without softmax, GPT-2's absolute position, Pythia's rotary) and a sixfold range of size all redundantly maintain the two variables through a super-additive middle. The exact size and redundancy of the front and back vary more from model to model — Pythia-160M, for instance, does most of its work at the back, and GPT-2-large's front resists a single joint class-and-position projection even though its layers pass one at a time — so it is the redundant maintenance middle, not the precise front/back split, that is the robustly shared shape.

A caveat added in review. The shape here — a super-additive, redundant middle where any one layer is dispensable but the band collectively matters — rests on ablation and is solid. The claim that what the middle keeps computing is specifically class-and-position rests on keep-only recovery, and a later control showed that recovery cannot isolate class-and-position from any subspace of the same rank (see "Why we don't put a number on it"). So read this section's percentages as describing a low-rank, redundant maintenance process; that the maintained content is grammatical class and position is supported elsewhere, by naming and steering, not by these recovery numbers.

One refinement on the word "maintenance": the middle is not merely refreshing the current token's class. Ask what the class each layer writes is a function of, and the front (layers 0–2) writes a class that is 73–88% predictable from the current token alone — its surface part-of-speech — while the middle writes a class that is only ~40% predictable from the token and mostly depends on the surrounding context (layer 5's write is just 22% token-derived). So the middle is contextualising grammar — computing a token's role in its sentence, not re-deriving its dictionary category. And it re-derives rather than carries because the residual is leaky (every block rescales it, so an early write decays) while the raw token is re-injected at every block, so each layer recomputes the grammatical information it needs, in the form it needs, from the always-available token plus the accumulating context.

Why is one layer high-rank and another low? Not because their encoders differ in capacity — the weights that read the residual are near-full-rank in every layer. The difference is in what they actually compute: a high-rank layer's gates fire a genuinely larger, more distinct set of features (block 1 realises ~2.4× as many as block 0), which its decoder then spreads across many outputs. High-rank layers are not doing the same thing many times; they detect more.

One caution came out of trying to push this further. A tempting story — that a high-rank layer is secretly a union of low-rank pieces, one per cluster of inputs — turned out to be false at a fair bar: cluster the tokens any way you like, and each cluster of a high-rank layer still needs almost the whole basis. The same test on a genuinely low-rank layer passes cleanly (its punctuation cluster is literally one direction), which is exactly how we know the high-rank layers are really high-rank and not an artifact of the method.

Circuits compose locally, and firing is not function

Information moves through the residual stream mostly between neighbours. Measuring how much each layer's output directions land in the next layer's input directions — and confirming it causally, by deleting a layer's output subspace and watching the next layer's gates move 9–44× more than for a random subspace — shows the dominant paths are layer i → block i+1, with the final block acting as a broad integration hub that reads from many earlier layers. Deleting a layer's write cannot change an earlier block's gates at all, and the measurement duly reads exactly zero there. Each MLP also reads one part of the residual and writes to a largely different part (the read and write subspaces are ~93% orthogonal), so the stream carries information forward through a succession of fresh subspaces rather than any layer amplifying its own input.

Putting circuits together this way forced a correction worth stating plainly. It is easy to name a component by when it fires — several components across the network spike on newlines and periods, which looks like a "sentence-boundary" circuit. But deleting them tells a different story: only one of them actually helps predict at boundaries. The most prominent "boundary" head fires on newlines yet, when removed, hurts prediction of ordinary content words far more than boundaries — it fires at boundaries but its job is writing the open-vocabulary continuation that follows. Firing is an input pattern; function is what breaks when you remove it, and only the second earns a circuit's name. Applying that test as a rule confirmed the two flagship circuits and dissolved a mirage:

  • The newline circuit is real and sharply localised. At period positions the model tells real line-ends from mid-sentence periods with AUC 0.81; delete the front two blocks' attention and that drops to 0.51 — pure chance — while a matched random deletion leaves it at 0.79. The front attention is the line-end discriminator.
  • The article circuit is real, and split as claimed. The a/an-vs-the choice (AUC 0.87) is carried by the front blocks — front attention more than front MLP, exactly the division we'd traced — and by nothing past block 5. It is a front decision, distributed across a handful of front components rather than any single one.
  • The "boundary circuit" was a firing illusion. Of the three components that fire at boundaries, only the last-block one is boundary-selective when deleted; the others do general continuation work and happen to trigger there.

Splitting a layer into parts that wire together

To read a layer you first have to break its big weight matrices into a small number of reusable parts, and then see how the parts in one layer feed the parts in the next. The textbook way to split a matrix orders its parts by how much energy they carry. On this model that is the wrong order: the loudest directions are the near-constant "massive activations" that carry almost none of the prediction, so if you keep only the top few parts and drop the rest you wreck the model's loss — at the second and third layers, keeping only the eight loudest parts is worse than deleting the layer entirely. The energy order and the loss order are not the same order.

A better split is to let the model learn an over-complete kit of parts and use only a handful on any one token — a sparse code. This stays faithful to the original weights and keeps the loss, and it recovers structure the energy split cannot. It beat the textbook split at every early layer we tried; where the textbook split went catastrophic, the sparse kit still recovered 50–60% of the layer's work from eight parts. This is the right family of decomposition for this model, and it holds all the way down the front of the network, not just at one layer.

The payoff is that these parts are read straight off the weights, with no data mixed in — so the wiring between one layer's parts and the next, which part feeds which, is fixed in the weights and identical no matter what text you show the model. We checked: the wiring from the sparse kit does not move at all when you swap the text, while the data-aware version of the textbook split moves by 150%. The sparsity — which few parts are active on a given token — is the routing that runs on top of that fixed wiring. One honest caveat: you can read the wiring from the weights, but predicting the exact signal that flows on a given token also needs the pieces sitting between the two matrices — a per-token rescale, and the attention step, which has no softmax and simply mixes across positions. Those are mostly gentle: with attention's position-mixing removed, a plain linear map already accounts for most of the same-position flow. The composition is largely linear; attention's blending of neighbours is the main thing a weights-only reading leaves out, not any harsh nonlinearity.

Left to themselves the parts wire together densely — each source part touches about one in seven targets. A clean, sparse wiring diagram is not something you get for free; you have to ask for it, by training the two layers' kits together with a penalty on the number of connections. On a hand-built test with a known wiring we could recover the true connections and nothing else — a deliberately wrong wiring scored near zero — but only once the connection penalty was strong enough; reconstruction alone leaves the wiring ambiguous. On the real model, training the two neighbouring matrices together this way cut the connections by roughly three-quarters while holding the loss.

That last step came with a trap worth stating plainly. Training the parts on the model's loss alone quietly ruins them: the loss does not care whether a part still reconstructs its matrix, so the parts drift to fit the handful of training sentences and their faithfulness collapses — reconstruction quality falls straight through zero even as you start from a good solution. It looks like it might be undertraining; it is the opposite, and more steps make it worse. The fix is to anchor the training with the reconstruction target alongside the loss, so the parts stay faithful while the loss and the sparsity penalty do their work. With the anchor, the joined-up decomposition keeps the model's loss at or above where the separate parts left it, keeps every part faithful, and still cuts the wiring by three-quarters — a faithful, loss-aware, sparsely-wired decomposition of two neighbouring layers, read as a small graph.

Where the interpretable structure actually lives

The obvious way to find a layer's "features" is to learn a big dictionary of parts and keep the handful each token uses. We did that, and then asked the question that decides whether you can trust the result: with no ground truth to check against, how do you know a part is real and not an artefact of the fit? The honest answer is that you triangulate — a real part should be stable when you refit from a new random start, causally load-bearing when you remove it, and interpretable as some consistent input. And on the learned parts, those three properties simply do not line up. Refit the dictionary from a different random seed and almost none of the individual parts come back (they recur at 0.4 on a scale where 1 is identical and 0.2 is chance). The parts back each other up, so removing one alone barely moves the loss while removing a group moves it far more. And a part's interpretability has almost nothing to do with how much it matters or how stable it is — the nameable parts are not the important ones. Grouping the parts by which fire together doesn't rescue any of this. The individual learned part is just the wrong unit.

The structure is there — it just isn't the learned parts, and you don't need to fit anything to see it. Take the layer's output and average it over every position where the current token is, say, "the"; the context washes out and you are left with the part of the output the token's identity drives. Do that for every token and look at the directions those averages spread along. That subspace is canonical — computed straight from the model and the data, it comes out the same on either half of the corpus (overlap 0.82, versus 0.40 for the learned parts), with no seed to depend on. It is causal, and dramatically so: delete that ~60-direction subspace from the first layer's output and the model's loss jumps by 1.3 nats, where deleting a random subspace of the same size costs almost nothing — a factor of 270. The reverse holds just as sharply: keep only those ~60 directions and throw the entire rest of the layer's output away, and the model still keeps 92% of what the layer was worth (a random 60 directions keep 8%) — the other thousand dimensions the layer writes are, for the loss, almost inert. And it is readable: the top directions are grammatical classes. One axis runs from determiners and articles ("the", "a", "an", "his") to the rest; another is punctuation; others are sentence-opening pronouns ("It", "He", "She"), auxiliary verbs ("is", "was", "were", "has"), digits, prepositions, and conditionals. The front of the network sorts what it writes by part of speech.

That also explains why the learned parts looked so unreliable. Their dictionary does sit largely inside this canonical subspace — but only covers about half of it, and picks an arbitrary set of axes within the part it covers, different every seed. They were a wobbly rotation of a solid thing. So the two tools have two jobs: the learned sparse dictionary is for faithful reconstruction of what a weight computes, and the token-averaged subspace is for the interpretable, causal, stable structure — and it is the second, not the first, that answers "what is this layer for" without any ground truth. Measured across depth, this token-class subspace is causally dominant at every layer we tested but its weight is concentrated up front (270× at the first layer, fading to 6× by layer twelve) — the same barbell as the rest of the model: the early layers do the grammatical sorting, the deep middle barely does anything.

None of this is peculiar to this one model. We ran the same test — average the first MLP's output over each token, keep only the directions those averages span, and see how much of the layer survives — on two ordinary pretrained transformers, GPT-2 and Pythia-410M. Both behave the same way: the very first MLP carries almost the entire MLP workload (in Pythia it is worth 7.3 nats of loss while every later layer is worth a fraction of one), and keeping only 64–128 token-class directions of that first layer recovers most of it — 0.93 of GPT-2's first layer at 128 directions, 0.76 of Pythia's, against 0.30 and 0.04 for a random subspace of the same size. The number drifts down a little as the models get larger — bigger networks spread the work somewhat wider — but the shape is the same everywhere: a trained transformer's first MLP writes a low-rank, token-class-organised summary, and you can read it straight off the activations without fitting anything. The grammatical-sorting front is a property of the architecture, not of this particular model.

The front is a class-and-position computer

Push the earlier finding one step and it stops being "the layer's output is low-rank" and becomes a statement about what the layer computes. The current token is already sitting in the residual stream, raw, the whole way up — so a per-token summary of an early layer's output cannot be there to record which token it is. What it records is the token's grammatical class, and that is something the layer actively works out. Average the first MLP's output over every appearance of each token and you get a table with the effective width of about twenty-three directions for hundreds of tokens — the layer has collapsed identity into a handful of class directions. That table is not a linear shadow of the embedding (a linear fit recovers under half of it), and it separates grammatical classes about 1.8× more cleanly than the raw embedding does. The first MLP takes token identity in and writes token class out — determiners together, numbers together, punctuation together — a computed abstraction, not a copy.

Position is the same story in miniature. The model never hands the MLP a position; position only reaches it through the rotary phases that attention applies to its queries and keys. Rotary is a high-dimensional object — dozens of frequencies — but what survives into the layer's output is tiny: a roughly two-dimensional, smooth "how far into the sequence" signal. It is absolute early-versus-late position, not an odd/even or short-period pattern, and it preserves the coarse shape of rotary while throwing away the fine frequencies. So the layer reads rotary and writes a low-resolution position readout, exactly as it reads identity and writes class.

Reading that position signal by which absolute positions load on it shows what "how far into the sequence" actually means to the model, and it is not linear. Its dominant direction tracks the logarithm of position (correlation 0.98, versus 0.79 for raw position): the model draws sharp distinctions among the first ten or twenty tokens and then compresses everything later, so positions 200 and 250 look nearly identical to it while 2 and 5 do not. Riding alongside are a sharp first-token landmark — a spike that fires only at the very start of the sequence — and a faint oscillating component, the last visible trace of the individual rotary frequencies. Fine early, coarse late, with a marker for the beginning: a sensible thing to compute if what matters is mostly how a span opens.

These two computed quantities — grammatical class and coarse position — are a large part of what the front of the network represents. A first pass suggested a clean number for how large: force every component to keep only its class-and-position content and the model still recovers ~78% of what the components contribute, against a random subspace's 4%. That looked like "the model is four-fifths a class-and-position computer." It isn't — and the correction matters enough to lead with. The 4% baseline used the wrong null: a random subspace ignores the data. The fair null is a subspace of the same rank built from shuffled class and position labels — same construction, same dimensionality, meaning removed. That null recovers 76%, essentially tying the real class-and-position subspace's 78%. In other words, any low-rank subspace of that size recovers ~78%, because a component's output is simply low-rank; the recovery number measures compressibility, not class-and-position specifically. So we withdraw "the model is 78% class-and-position" as a measurement of that variable's share — keep-only recovery cannot isolate it.

What survives is the part that never depended on that number. The low-rank structure genuinely is grammatical class and position — established two ways a shuffled subspace cannot fake. First, naming: read the strongest directions by their tokens and they are parts of speech (determiners, pronouns, numbers, punctuation…), while the shuffled control is incoherent. Second, and decisively, causation: steering the class-and-position content moves the prediction toward the injected class specifically — inject "and" and predictions move toward what follows "and", not toward what follows "a" or "." — while a random direction of the same size does not, and the effect is a class-by-class match, not a generic nudge. The model computes a low-rank representation, and that representation is — by name and by class-specific causal test — grammatical class and log-position. We just can't put a trustworthy "fraction of the model" on it with the keep-only tool. GPT-2 and Pythia behave the same way: their first layers, read by name, carve out the same grammatical categories, and their loss divides the same way (below). Alongside the class-and-position structure there is a genuinely distributed residue — context-dependent computation with no low-rank handle — that shows up wherever the model does something conditional; how big it is we state through the loss, not through keep-only recovery.

Why we don't put a number on it. It is tempting to quote a fraction — "the model is N% class-and-position" — and we did, until the null caught up with us. The keep-only recovery does have real properties: the subspace generalizes (build it on one half of the text, score on a held-out half, and the recovery is unchanged), and single-component scores are inflated by large constant offsets so the all-at-once measurement is stricter. But the deeper problem is that keep-only recovery cannot isolate class-and-position at all. A subspace of the same rank built from shuffled labels recovers just as much, because a component's output is low-rank and any subspace of that rank captures its dominant directions. The recovery number measures compressibility, not this variable's share. So there is no honest "fraction of the model" to report here. The evidence that the structure is class-and-position is categorical, not quantitative: you can read the directions as parts of speech, and you can steer predictions with them. That is what the next sections rest on.

Everything so far shows class-and-position is necessary — remove it and the model gets worse. The stronger claim is that it is sufficient: that you can drive the prediction by editing it. You can. Take the direction that separates one token-class from average, add it (amplified) to the early layers, and the model's next-token distribution moves toward what that class is typically followed by — push the "indefinite-article" class and predictions slide toward what follows "a"; the same for "and". A random direction of the same size does the opposite, degrading the prediction. Two honest caveats: the edit has to be amplified to bite, because the model keeps re-deriving class-and-position from the token embedding it never discards, so a single gentle nudge is overwritten; and it works cleanly for some classes and barely moves others (the ultra-frequent "the" is already the default, so there is nowhere to push it). Necessary, generalizing to held-out text, and steerable — the class-and-position variable is the model's real, low-dimensional, causal computation, not just a pattern we read off it.

So what are the classes? Reading the strongest directions of the class code by the tokens that load on them, they turn out to be ordinary grammatical categories — the model has, in effect, rediscovered parts of speech. A shuffled-label control makes the same directions incoherent, so this is real structure, not pattern-matching on an arbitrary basis:

  • Articles & determiners — the, a, an, his, their
  • Pronouns — capitalized (You, He, We, They) and lowercase (she, he, him) — and a direction that puts pronouns at one end and numbers at the other
  • Numbers / digits — 13, 14, 15, 28
  • Punctuation & sentence boundaries — ., ), !, .", newline
  • Conjunctions — and, And, &
  • Contraction suffixes — 'll, 've, 'd
  • Sentence-initial quantifiers / focus words — Many, Some, Not, Even
  • Isolated capital letters (initials, abbreviations) — a class of their own

That is the whole story of the class variable in one list: the model spends most of its first six layers sorting the current token into a grammatical bucket like these, alongside a coarse sense of position, and reads that back out later. (A caveat on precision: the strongest dozen-or-so directions are these clean categories; underneath them is a longer tail of finer, more lexical distinctions, so "grammatical class" names the dominant, causal part, not every last dimension.)

And this is not one model's quirk. Running the same read-out on GPT-2 and Pythia turns up the very same grammatical axes in their first layers — determiners, conjunctions, punctuation, and clean verb and preposition classes (GPT-2 separates the be-verbs is/are/was; Pythia separates the prepositions to/in/on/for). Three different architectures independently sort their first-layer tokens into parts of speech. Whatever else these models are doing, they all start by learning grammar.

And you can watch that grammar get used. Tag every token by its part of speech and ask, for each class, what class the model expects next — then compare to what actually follows in real text. They line up almost exactly (a mismatch of 0.009 nats, versus 0.056 when the tags are shuffled). The expected transitions are the ones any grammar would predict: after a determiner the model wants a noun 82% of the time; after a preposition, a noun or another determiner (word 48%, the/a 34%); after a pronoun, a verb (74%); after a number, punctuation (52%). So the back of the network spends the class variable on grammatical sequencing — given the current word's part of speech, predict a grammatically valid next part of speech. Predicting the class of the next token is nearly solved; the hard, distributed work that is left is choosing which word inside that class — which is exactly the residue that carries the model's uncertainty.

That split can be measured exactly. Break the model's per-token loss into the part spent getting the grammatical class right and the part spent choosing the specific word within it (the two add up to the whole, by the chain rule). Grammar costs 0.75 nats — 23%; the word-within-the-class costs 2.48 nats — 77%. This is a clean split of the loss and it holds exactly (the two add to the whole by the chain rule) and universally (GPT-2 and Pythia below). It is tempting to now map it onto the representation — "class-and-position is the easy grammatical quarter" — but we resist that, for the same reason as above: attributing a fraction of either loss component to the class-and-position representation would need keep-only recovery, and keep-only cannot isolate that representation from any other subspace of the same rank. So we report the loss split as a fact about the task — grammar is the cheap quarter, specific-word choice the expensive three-quarters — without claiming a matching share for the representation. What we can say about the hard three-quarters comes from the loss directly, not from recovery: it is largely irreducible.

How much of that hard part is even learnable? Watching the two components as context accumulates answers it. The grammar cost is essentially paid by the first few tokens and barely moves after — it needs almost no context. The word-choice cost does fall as context builds, by about 1.1 nats from the start of a passage to deep inside one, so context genuinely helps pick the word — but it levels off around 2.4 nats within this model. It is tempting to call that floor "irreducible entropy — language itself," and an earlier draft did; but that is an overclaim, and the check is easy. 2.4 nats is this model's residual, not a measured entropy. Feed the same text to larger models and the word-choice cost keeps falling: 2.74 nats in GPT-2 small, 2.51 in medium, 2.39 in GPT-2 large — a steady drop with scale, so a stronger model genuinely does make better word choices. The honest reading is in between: most of the word-choice cost is hard to reduce (the bulk survives even a 774M model), but it is not a wall — a real slice (roughly a nat-tenth here, and still shrinking with scale) is structure a bigger model captures and this one leaves on the table. So the difficulty that remains after class-and-position is mostly, but not entirely, the inherent entropy of language; the reducible remainder is diffuse content-selection we simply don't have a clean handle on (yet).

We can now say what that hard floor is made of, token by token. Score every token with both bilin18 and GPT-2 large and the two agree remarkably — their per-token losses correlate 0.90, so the two models find the same tokens hard, and 87% of bilin18's loss is a floor the big model pays too. And that floor has a sharp signature: it is almost entirely first mentions. A token whose type has not yet appeared in the passage costs about 4.2 nats to predict; one that has already appeared costs about 1.7 — the same split for both models. The hardest quartile is 89% first-mention, 82% content words: AP, tornado, regions, March. So "irreducible" is not a blank wall — it is the choice of a new content word, and the content machine has already done real work on it (the new word is topically apt — tornado in a weather story), but the exact word is a genuinely open choice that even a 774M model cannot pin down. The remaining difficulty is topic-constrained, first-mention word choice: we understand the constraint; the residual is the open pick. (One caveat: GPT-2 large is trained on WebText, so it is slightly out-of-distribution on this FineWeb text, which makes the model-to-model gap a lower bound — the correlation and the first-mention signature are robust to it.)

And this division is not particular to this model. Split the loss the same way for GPT-2 and Pythia and the numbers barely move: the word-within-the-class share is 77% here, 75% in GPT-2, 76% in Pythia. Three architectures, trained separately, all spend about a quarter of their loss on grammar and three-quarters on lexical choice. The shape of the problem — a small, cleanly-interpretable grammatical core wrapped around a large, hard-to-reduce lexical remainder — appears to be a property of the task, not of any one network (with the remainder only partly reducible by scale, as above).

That residue turns out to have a job, and it is the opposite of what you might guess. Sort every prediction by how hard it was — how much loss the full model took — and ask how much of each bucket the class-and-position skeleton alone recovers. On the model's hardest, most uncertain predictions the skeleton recovers essentially everything: when the model does not know what comes next, all it has to offer is the grammatical class and the position, and there is nothing more to strip away. On its easiest, most confident predictions the skeleton recovers only half. So the distributed fifth is not there for the hard cases — it is the specific content that turns a grammatical expectation ("a noun is coming") into a confident bet on the actual word. Class and position are the skeleton the model always has; the diffuse remainder is the flesh that lets it be sure. We understand the skeleton completely and causally; the flesh is the part that still has no low-rank handle.

One caution earned the hard way. It is tempting to go further and name individual heads — this one is "the determiner head," that one is "the position head" — and the folded weights do show, for instance, that one early head's query-key geometry is dominated by determiners. But deleting that head hurts prediction roughly evenly across every word class, not especially at determiners: it is a broad, multi-purpose mover whose determiner-flavoured geometry is a small part of its job. The honest unit is the variable a component reads and writes, not a one-word label for the head. Class and position are real, computed, and reused; "head number four does nouns" is usually a story the geometry tells and the interventions refuse.

The 2→3→4 chain got its abstraction treatment, and the answer is the anticipated negative, now measured: the 3→4 edge has no small variable set — eight weights-derived coupling directions carry 21% of the full-edge effect (registered bar: half), and the coupling operator beats the writer's own loudest directions by only ~8%. The chain's content is genuinely high-rank. The syntax bus, meanwhile, has no upstream supplier: no layer 5–15 moves L16's bus span more than a random span (ratios ≤1.15) — at ten layers' range every influence saturates into the same diffuse ~0.2σ floor, so the L16→L17 relay is local structure assembled from unstructured supply. The bus content itself is old, linearly exposed, and lexical-headed: the stream entering L16 predicts it at R² 0.979 with a purely linear map, two-thirds of its variance is already determined at L2 — and over half comes from the current token's embedding alone, so "syntax bus" overreads; it is a lexical channel with contextual refinement. The model, in one sentence: five genuinely nonlinear front layers, a nearly-linear thirteen-layer pipe, and one quadratic interaction skin at the end. Inside L1 itself the story splits spectrally: the top-48 interface every reader consumes is nearly linearly generable (12% of the layer's replacement cost at 59% of its energy), the deep tail beyond rank 512 is inert, and the functionally necessary nonlinearity concentrates in a mezzanine band of mid-variance directions (ranks ~50–500) that none of the reading experiments — all conducted on the top-48 — ever touched. The attention-pattern census closed the last unprofiled component family with a triple-negative: patterns are weakly positional (only L2 hosts a positional hub), not at all lexical (key-token identity explains zero, at every depth), and therefore genuinely contextual — attention is the one component of this model that is not token-headed.
