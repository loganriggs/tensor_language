Ten experiments on toy networks whose right answer is known in advance, an adversarial review that forced three retractions, and a mathematical pass that demoted two of the "findings" to theorems. This is where all three stand, what the results mean, and — new in this revision — what happened when the same measurements were finally run on the 546M-parameter model itself.

The setup, and the words

Everything here concerns one kind of network layer. It takes an input, applies two different linear maps to it, multiplies the two results together entry by entry, and mixes the outcome down to an output. That multiplication is the whole point: it is what lets the layer compute things like "is A true and is B true".

The interaction tables. Because every output is a sum of products of pairs of input numbers, each output is exactly a quadratic function of the input — and any quadratic function can be written with one square table of numbers. So the whole layer, however it was parameterised, is captured by one table per output. This matters because the same layer can be written down in endlessly many ways: rotate the internal representation, compensate elsewhere, and every weight changes while the function does not. The tables don't change. Everything below is a statement about the tables.

Gauge. A rewriting of the weights that leaves the function alone. Any quantity that changes under a gauge is a fact about the notation, not the network. One of our own measurements turned out to be gauge-dependent, and had to be withdrawn.

What the layer ignores. If moving the input in some direction leaves every output untouched, the layer is blind to it. That is readable off the tables and checkable by nudging the input and watching nothing happen.

Recovery. Most of the work asks a single question: can you find the independent pieces of a computation from the weights alone, without being told the answer? We only ever ask it where the answer is known, so that "the method said X" can be scored rather than believed.

The four sceptical baselines. Every experiment is re-run against an untrained network (does the method find structure in noise?), the same trained network rewritten in a scrambled internal basis (does the finding survive, as it must, if it is about the function?), a network trained on shuffled labels (is this structure from the task or from training in general?), and a shuffle of which output reads which piece.

The experiments cover: what a layer ignores; a network learning modular arithmetic; when a decomposition into parts can be trusted; when a curved computation can be straightened; work shared between several consumers; routing through two layers; and four variants of multiplicative attention. Ten in all, each with a planted answer.

Highlight 1 — a circuit you can cut out of the weights

A network learning (a + b) mod 23 memorises first and generalises much later — the phenomenon called grokking. The correct solution is known: it works in waves, eleven frequencies, each in its own slice of the input space.

Delete everything in the weights outside those slices. No data, no retraining, no gradients — just zero out part of the tables.

Deleting a third of the function makes the model strictly better. Run the deleted part on its own and you see why:

Running the same deletion on every checkpoint through training shows when the good part appeared.

Careful with this one. An earlier version of this report said the circuit was "fully formed" at step 1500 and that grokking is the memorised part decaying away. Both were wrong and are retracted. At step 1500 the clean part accounts for only a quarter of what the model computes, and it keeps changing afterwards; what is right that early is the ranking, not the circuit. And the memorised part does not decay — it grows, then partly recedes. Grokking here is the circuit growing louder, not the memorisation growing quieter.

A second, cheaper edit works too. Since a + b = b + a, the correct solution treats the two input slots interchangeably; the trained networks don't, by about 13%. Averaging each network with its own slot-swapped copy — again pure weight editing, and assuming far less than knowing the answer is made of waves — also takes all three to 100%, and roughly doubles how much of the structure the analysis can find unaided.

Highlight 2 — most of the tools don't work, and we can say which

The single most useful output of the programme is not a discovery but a scorecard. Every method was run against a task whose answer was known, so its failures are measurable rather than arguable.

Two failures are worth spelling out because they were the plan's main instruments.

The "genuine conjunction" test fires on everything. The idea was that a head really computing "A and B" would show each half being individually vague and the combination sharp. It does show that — and so does a head on a task where one property alone is sufficient and there is nothing to combine. It fired on 16 of 16 models across all three architecture variants. In the variant the real 546M model actually uses, it fires on randomly initialised weights. Multiplying two things sharpens the result whatever they mean.

Deleting one half of a head is not a well-defined operation. The plan proposed removing one factor and checking performance falls. Three ways of doing that all fail a basic soundness check: on a task where the surviving factor demonstrably suffices to score 100%, every version of the deletion drops the model to 27–59%. The output depends on the two halves multiplied, so removing one doesn't leave the other's computation behind — it leaves a differently shaped object with no honest baseline.

What does work is asking what each part reads, rather than what breaks when you remove it.

Highlight 3 — routing is recoverable, the split inside a head is not

The final experiment stacks everything: a layer that computes three features from tokens, a two-part attention head that should read one feature in each part, and a separate path that should read the third. The whole routing table is known in advance.

Two claims came out of the first six networks. The division of labour inside the two-part head is not stable — that one survived. The other, that routing between paths is reliable, did not.

updated The full run finished at sixteen networks and split the two claims apart. The instability is confirmed and quantified: the division ranges over a factor of twenty, and six of the sixteen put both halves on the same property while still solving the task perfectly. But my own accompanying claim — that routing between paths is robust everywhere — is refuted by the same data. Four of the sixteen show no path separation at all (the 1× figures under the chart). Between-path routing is the more stable of the two, but it is not reliable either.

This matters for the real model because the plan proposes a per-head census of its 162 heads, classifying each by how its two halves divide the work. If that division is not a stable property of the architecture, the census is asking a question with no consistent answer — while a routing ledger between paths should be reliable.

Highlight 4 — two "findings" were theorems

Writing out the mathematics behind each experiment demoted two headline results. Both are still true and useful; neither was a measurement.

Straightening. A bilinear layer multiplies things, but if some piece barely varies over the inputs that actually occur, you can replace that multiplication with a straight-line approximation and store half as many numbers. The plan predicted this pays off for the middle-sized pieces. It does pay off — at a fixed budget it beats keep-or-delete at 22 of 25 budgets, by up to ten times — but the prediction about which pieces was wrong.

And the algebra says it had to come out that way: the ratio of "error after straightening" to "error after deleting" is identically unchanged when you scale a piece up or down, because both quantities scale together. The correlation with size is not approximately zero, it is exactly zero by construction. The experiment confirmed arithmetic.

The sceptical baselines then delivered a second correction: a randomly initialised network shows the same straightening advantage, more strongly than the trained one. So this is a compression technique with a correct selection rule — a property of quadratic functions on a given input distribution — not a discovery about what training builds.

Highlight 5 — when a decomposition into parts can be trusted

This experiment exists to bound the others. Build a teacher out of K known independent pieces, train a student, try to recover the pieces.

At 48 pieces the reconstruction is perfect to machine precision while a third of the recovered pieces are simply wrong. "It fits beautifully" is not evidence that the parts mean anything.

An initial version of this attributed the breakdown to the ratio of pieces to input dimensions. The reviewer pointed out the experiment could not tell that apart from the ratio to output dimensions, and a follow-up sweep showed the reviewer was right: at 32 pieces in 16 input dimensions, doubling the number of outputs turns total failure into complete recovery. The relevant limit is a classical bound from tensor decomposition, and by that bound the real 546M model's layers sit far outside any uniqueness guarantee — 4608 pieces against a limit of 1727.

Red teaming

An independent adversarial review went through every claim, every script and every stored number. It found real errors. Its verdict on fabrication was clean — every number it checked matched to the printed precision — but it found selection and framing problems, and three claims that were simply wrong.

retractedwhat was actually true
"Grokking is the memorised part decaying away" It grows to a peak and ends 28% above where it started. The circuit grows 3.1×. I had the mechanism backwards.
"The circuit is fully formed at step 1500" It accounts for 25% of the function there and keeps changing. The ranking is right early; the circuit is not finished.
"Deleting one half of a head shows it is inert" The deletion was invalid — negative on 56% of examples, inverting the head's ordering. No valid version exists.

Six further corrections followed, the sharpest being that a statistic I presented as an indictment of the trained model ("a third of its weights can't affect the function") is in fact better than chance — a random layer scores worse on the same measure, and I had not computed the baseline.

Two more errors I caught myself while responding, both worth recording because they are the same failure mode: I stated that bilin18's normalisation layer was essential on the strength of a measurement, before running the control that refuted it; and I wrote in the review-response document that three unreproducible numbers were "now in a2_followups.py" before writing that file. Claiming a fix before making it, in the document whose purpose is honest accounting, is exactly what the review was catching.

Everything is now closed: all retractions marked inline, every previously unreproducible number regenerated by committed code, and the machinery's self-checks extended from 12 to 20 — which promptly found a real accuracy ceiling in one of the decomposition routines that I recorded rather than tuned away.

The question we could not answer

One thing resisted five attempts. A trained model's leftover mess defeats structure-finding where artificial noise of exactly the same size does not — by a factor of two to three. Understanding why would be the difference between recovering a circuit and half-recovering it.

Tested and refuted: that the mess is bigger than it looks; that it repeats the same pattern across outputs; that it concentrates on a few pairs of blocks; that it is aligned with the signal; that it is distributed like the signal. One mechanism was found along the way that is real but not the culprit — a perturbation that repeats across outputs is devastating at a size where an independent one is harmless.

The most informative single result was a control: move the real leftover mess onto a completely unrelated signal and it is just as damaging. That rules out every explanation phrased as a relationship between the mess and the signal, which is what four of the five attempts had been. The last attempt searched directly for the guilty component and found there isn't one — no single part, no special small set, and the parts that most help when removed are nearly harmless on their own.

So the honest answer is negative, and the space is much smaller than when we started: not size, not repetition, not concentration, not any relationship to the signal, and no compact culprit. This is where guessing should stop. There is a practical workaround already — the symmetry edit described above recovers 7 to 11 of the 11 pieces with no ground truth used anywhere.

What this says about the real model

The 546M-parameter model these toys were built for uses exactly this layer — verified in its source, not its documentation. So the tools apply literally rather than by analogy. Its attention, however, is a variant the research plan does not list: an unnormalised product with no softmax anywhere, whose values are routinely negative. Every statistic in the plan's proposed head census is an entropy of a probability distribution, and that pattern is not one — so the census as written cannot be run on the model it was designed for.

Correction to the previous revision. It said the four proposed measurements were "not runnable here — the checkpoint is not on this machine." That was wrong. The checkpoint was merely not yet downloaded. It now is, and the rest of this page is what the measurements returned.

Real model 1 the recorded attention evidence

A statistic that fires on 100% of heads — and on 100% of random ones

The existing analysis of this model records that in every one of its 162 attention heads, combining the two score matrices produces a sharper pattern than either alone. That was read as evidence the two branches genuinely work together: each narrows what the other proposes.

The control is to run the identical measurement on the identical architecture with untrained weights. It fires on 162 of 162 there too — with a larger sharpening effect than the trained model shows (0.207 against 0.097). A second recorded number, that about half the pattern mass is negative, is 0.490 in the trained model and 0.500 in the random one.

Both recorded statistics are consequences of multiplying two matrices together, not of anything the model learned. They should not be cited as evidence about this model's attention.

Real model 2 what the data can even see

The middle of the network is built to ignore the thing its input is made of

A bilinear layer's behaviour for one output is captured by a symmetric matrix — a table of how strongly each pair of input directions interacts. Only the part of that table the data actually probes can affect the network's behaviour; the rest is dead weight left over from initialisation. In the toys, roughly two thirds of the table was live. The question was how much survives at real scale, where the table has 664,128 independent entries.

The first measurement said "under 1%, no better than a random matrix" — but that number turned out to be an artefact of sampling, not a fact about the weights. The fix is to vary the number of samples and read the shape of the curve rather than its height.

An unrelated matrix scores exactly 1× at every sample count, and the dashed null confirms the measurement is calibrated. Against that, layers 5 and 13 come in at 0.10× and 0.19× — an order of magnitude below an unrelated matrix. Scoring below chance is not "no structure". It means these layers are actively avoiding the directions the data uses most.

Checking why produced the cleanest result of the whole program. The input to each middle layer's bilinear unit, after normalisation, is almost a single fixed vector: at layers 5 through 7, every token at every position arrives within about 14° of the same direction. And the layer's response along that direction is essentially zero.

Two monotone trends telling one story. Where the input is most concentrated, the layer is most blind to it — reading only the small variation around the dominant direction. The first layer, whose input is the raw embedding and genuinely spread out, does the opposite and is five times more responsive there than chance; the last two layers swing back the same way.

The near-constant input is a known phenomenon in transformers. What is new here is that the layers are shaped around it — and the consequence: any measurement that weighs a layer's table by raw size is mostly measuring weight the data never touches. That invalidates a family of statistics used throughout the toy experiments, and the toys could not have caught it, because their inputs have no dominant direction by construction.

Real model 3 the payoff

Eight numbers in place of sixteen million parameters

The fix implied above is to weigh directions by how much the data uses them before ranking them. Tested against the alternative on every layer, that reweighting is worth a factor of 1.5 to 5 in how compactly a layer can be described. It also exposed a mistake worth flagging: measuring these tables along random output directions overstates their complexity by two to eight times. Along the directions a layer's output actually occupies, most layers need 32 to 64 terms.

The last layer needs four. That made it the one place in the model where the toys' machinery had enough signal per parameter to be pointed at something real. So its entire bilinear unit was deleted and replaced by four output directions carrying two squared projections each — eight terms, about 13,800 numbers standing in for roughly 15.9 million parameters — and the whole model re-scored on held-out text.

Deleting the layer's quadratic part costs 1.08 nats. The eight-term replacement costs 9.5% of that — and of those 9.5 points, 8.8 come from confining the output to four directions and only 0.7 from squashing those four down to two terms each. So the hard part is choosing the directions; once chosen, going to two terms is nearly free. Spending the identical budget the naive way — ranking by size instead of by how much the data uses each direction — does seven times the damage. That is the reweighting argument confirmed in the only currency that counts.

Correction. An earlier version of this page said the replacement "gives back all but 0.7%". That is the wrong quantity — 0.7% is the extra damage from the rank cut alone, not the cost of the whole replacement, which is 9.5%. Choosing the number of output directions by what it costs, rather than by a rule fixed in advance, also showed that the second-to-last layer, not the last, is the model's most compressible one: same 13,800 numbers, 4.2% damage against 9.5%. The rank finding stands; the compression headline was pointed at the wrong layer.

And the eight terms are readable

A symmetric table is a signed sum of squares, so the layer literally takes a few projections of the running state, squares them, and adds them with signs. The second output direction writes toward determiners — the, their, our, a, your. It subtracts a squared projection onto sentence-closing punctuation and adds one onto copulas and pronouns. Read as a rule: after a full stop, suppress "a determiner comes next"; when a copula or pronoun is present, boost it.

Naming a direction by the words it points at is the standard shortcut, and it is weak evidence — it assumes the direction the layer reads is the direction the output vocabulary writes. So the names were checked against what actually makes each feature fire, across 262,144 positions of real text, against a shuffled null.

All six clear the null, but unevenly, and "six out of six confirmed" would overstate it: the three leading features are solid, while the weakest sits at 0.114 against a chance level of 0.06 and its name should be treated as unverified. Five of six correlate better with the token actually present than with the one being predicted — these are detectors of what is there, not of what comes next.

The honest limit: this worked because the last layer happens to be nearly four-dimensional. The middle layers need 32 to 64 terms across four to eight times as many output directions — two orders of magnitude more, and not readable this way. That is the next thing to attack.

A first version of this verification is recorded in the notes as having failed: it used a text sample so small that only 48 words cleared the counting threshold, which put the chance level above the measured value. It established nothing and was rerun with 21× more data rather than reported.

Real model 4 where it stops working

The middle layers look free one at a time, and cost 2.9× that together

The obvious next question was whether the last layer was the start of a pattern. It is not. Running the same replacement on all eighteen layers, only two of them compress at all — and the reason turned out to be more interesting than "the rest are more complicated".

Two things stand out. The first layer is the most important bilinear unit in the model by a wide margin — deleting it costs 5.65 nats where nothing else costs more than 1.8. And everything from layer 2 to layer 15 looks almost free: under 0.52 nats each, most of them under 0.06. Nothing in the toy work predicted that concentration and nobody had looked for it.

"Almost free" is a trap, though, and the cheapest possible check exposes it. Delete all fourteen middle layers' bilinear units at once instead of one at a time:

Nearly three times the damage the parts predict. When one middle layer is removed the other thirteen absorb the loss; when all fourteen go there is nothing left to absorb it. So any claim that a particular layer "contributes almost nothing", based on removing it by itself, is unsafe in this model — and the chart above is exactly such a claim.

This also explains the compression result rather than just bounding it. The two layers that compress are precisely the two whose individual contribution is large. The ones that resist are those that matter little alone and a great deal together — which is what computation spread across depth looks like, rather than computation sitting in a layer.

A promised improvement to existing code that isn't one

The remaining item on the list was a proposed fix to code already in the repository, described in an earlier revision as the most actionable of the four. Reading that code first corrected the premise: it does not rank features "by size" as claimed — it already weights them by how much the data uses them, the same correction validated above.

But it did expose a genuine defect. The approximation is built around a quantity whose average was never subtracted, so the fit has to spend part of its budget reproducing a constant that could have been stored for free. That omitted constant turns out to carry 16 to 52% of the relevant energy, so the diagnosis looked substantial.

Fixing it buys almost nothing: seven of twelve settings improve, by an average of 0.0045 nats, and the single best case is a 3% relative gain at the very smallest budget. Beyond that budget it is a wash in both directions, and the predicted link between the size of the win and the size of the constant is absent. The reason is mundane — the approximation method absorbs a single constant direction nearly for free, so it only matters when the budget is already too small to work.

Diagnosis right, predicted consequence wrong. Worth doing as tidiness, not a result. Closed negative.

Real model 5 the last of the four tests

A number that ranges from 9% to 64% on the same unchanged layer

The final test targeted the strongest existing claim about this model. Earlier work found that removing 32 directions from its second layer together does far more damage than removing them one at a time and adding up — individual directions explained 24% of the effect, the rest appeared only jointly — and read that as evidence the layer's computation is irreducibly spread out, the point where naming one direction at a time stops working. The toy work predicted this was an artefact of the method used to pick the directions, and that a sparse dictionary would do much better.

It does not. At the original budget of 32 directions, principal directions explain 9% individually, a random rotation of the same subspace 9% (which reproduces that work's own control exactly), a matched-size dictionary 13%, and a large dictionary's best 32 atoms 21%. Four fifths of the effect stays joint-only under every basis tried. The claim survives the challenge, which is a better outcome for it than the challenge succeeding would have been.

But matching the comparison properly — equalising how much of the layer's output each set removes, which the original test design asked for and my first script skipped — produced something neither prediction anticipated.

The fraction is not a property of the layer. It ranges from 64% down to 9% on the same untouched weights, driven almost entirely by how many directions you remove at once. Going from 4 directions to 32 multiplies the joint damage by fourteen and the summed individual damage by only 1.4 — interference grows much faster than the number of things being removed.

So "76% appears only under joint removal" is largely a statement about removing 32 things simultaneously, not a scale-free measure of how distributed the layer is. The figure needs its budget attached to mean anything — and this is the second time in this batch that a superadditive ablation produced a misleading headline, the first being the middle layers looking free one at a time.

My own prediction failed here too, and failed because the first version of the script omitted the very control the test called for. At equal removed output the ordering reverses: principal directions come out three times more individually attributable than dictionary atoms, not less.

Real model 6 the through-line, resolved

Neither a smear nor a star: about ten directions run the layer

Every misleading number in this batch had the same root: what removing a piece costs depends on how much else was removed with it. There is a standard attribution that is immune to this by construction — average each direction's marginal effect over every possible order of removal, which forces the parts to sum to the whole exactly, leaving no "unexplained" residual to misread. It costs 660 model evaluations. We paid.

The answer for the disputed second layer: its causal content is carried by a handful of effective directions — the biggest holds over a quarter of the layer by itself, the top eight hold about seventy percent, and the tail matters little. Both earlier readings now have a diagnosis. Removing directions one at a time said "one direction and 91% mystery" — blind to interference. The original "irreducibly distributed" phrasing said "an even smear" — an artefact of the 32-at-once budget. The layer is neither: a small oligarchy with a clear leader.

The fair-share ranking also shows solo ablation doesn't just understate — it misranks. The leading direction is worth 4.6× what removing it alone suggests, and two directions whose solo removal looks actively helpful each carry a genuine +0.019 nats. Every ranking in the existing analyses of this model that was built on remove-one-at-a-time inherits this distortion.

Correction from the sample-size control. This measurement was first run on a basis fit from 33k positions and gave "about ten" effective directions with a 27% leader. Refitting the basis on 4.8× the data and rerunning everything: five to six effective directions, leader at 39%. The count was inflated by basis noise — the small sample split one real component into two directions, and the attribution spread its mass across both, so under-sampling makes a layer look more distributed than it is. The two top directions and their names are stable across sample sizes (cosines 0.985 and 0.946); the oligarchy-with-a-leader verdict sharpens rather than weakens. The same control showed the in-sample "70% of the layer" framing was about double the held-out figure — from document heterogeneity, not overfitting (a weight-derived basis that fits nothing drops by the same factor) — so between-basis ratios on this page are trustworthy and absolute "share of the layer" percentages are row-group-relative.

One methods note: with full permutations the parts summing to the whole is an identity, so that check validates bookkeeping, not accuracy — the honest error bars are the per-direction ones (±0.004–0.014 nats), and the leading structure is far outside them.

updated The natural next question — are these ten causal directions the same ones a dictionary can name? — has now been run, and closes a loop. Earlier analysis of this model concluded that naming and causing are nearly orthogonal: a dictionary names the layer's parts, but none of them carries causal load. That causal side was measured by remove-one-at-a-time — the instrument just shown to misrank. Re-asked with the fair-share ranking, dictionary atoms hold 1.8× more of their energy in the span of the ten causally-leading directions than the ten trailing ones, above the 95th percentile of a random-subset control, and the effect strengthens for the most-used atoms. The nameable structure lives where the causal mass lives; what survives of the orthogonality claim is only that no single atom is load-bearing — interference among ten real parts, not a divorce between naming and causing. (An enrichment of 1.8×, not identity; one layer of one model.)

Four layers read end to end — and the weights knew

Highlights of the loop, each with its control:

Causal verification split a story in half. Layer 1's lead direction correlates with document register (its firing is half document-identity by variance, and it tracks layout characters). The compression half verified: a 2,308-parameter replacement restores the model to within noise, while the same replacement built on a random direction repairs nothing. The semantics half failed: deleting the direction hurts prose most, and injecting layout characters into prose moves it no more than injecting random words. The exact attribution that named layout tokens was decomposing natural covariance — an exact decomposition is not an intervention, and that lesson is now load-bearing across the program.

The variable graph holds where it should. Stated as variables — head 4's context feeds a scalar z, the direction writes a·z²+b — and tested by transplanting values between different documents: moving head 4's context moves z (79% of all movement; no other head above 14%), and transplanting z reproduces 68% of the true coefficient-transplant's downstream effect against a 13% shuffled control. The 92%-versus-68% gap is localised to cross-document transport, which is exactly where the earlier transfer test said the fit degrades.

One anomaly, kept visible. Layer 16's replacement lands below the intact model's loss. The discarded 664k parameters actively hurt on this evaluation set. Registered hypothesis: train/eval distribution shift — the compressed core generalises, the remainder is distribution-specific. Untested until matching training-distribution data is available.

Then the theory pass inverted the whole pipeline. The bilinear layer is a third-order tensor, and its output structure has a closed form from the weights plus exactly one data statistic (the input second moment). That formula holds 90–99.6% of the measured causal leader at all four depths (random: ~1%), and pure weight algebra ranks the responsible attention head first with a 5× margin. Everything expensive the program discovered about these leaders was one computation away from the weights. Going forward the order reverses: weight-side candidates first, model evaluations only to verify and to test edges.

Also in this arc: the layer-1 investigation was corrected twice by its own controls — a sample-size rerun collapsed "ten directions" to five-to-six (small-sample PCA splits one real component into two), and a first ablation run silently scored the intact model and was caught because every rung agreed. Both are in the record.

Honest ledger

Ten experiments, all with baselines. Two of the research plan's predictions measured wrong and corrected; two more of its proposed instruments shown not to work at all; one of its experiments shown to be unidentifiable as specified. Three retractions from external review and two self-caught. Forty-seven mathematical propositions verified, two of which demoted our own findings. One question attacked five times and answered negatively.

The single most reusable output is the scorecard: on problems where the answer is known, we now know which of these methods can be believed and at what noise level they stop working. That is what the toys were for.

On the real model, three of the four planned tests are done and five follow-ups they forced are done with them. One headline number of my own needed correcting by an order of magnitude, and does not survive in its original form. One recorded piece of evidence about the model's attention has been shown to carry no information. One family of statistics used throughout the toy work has been shown not to transfer, with the reason measured. One registered prediction of mine failed. One verification attempt had no statistical power and was rerun rather than reported. And one layer of the model has been replaced by eight numbers and read.
