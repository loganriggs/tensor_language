Architecture

The bilinear layer, and what "folding" means

Before the method, the object. Everything rests on one choice: the model multiplies instead of thresholding. Start with a single unit, then the whole layer, then why that makes it foldable.

Start simple: one bilinear unit is a product of two knobs

A normal neuron takes one weighted sum and thresholds it. A bilinear unit takes two weighted sums of the same input and multiplies them — no threshold. That product is a soft AND: large only when both reads are large, and it can flip sign.
# one bilinear unit: two linear reads, multiplied
score_L = l · x          # "is it about music?"
score_R = r · x          # "is it a plural noun?"
out     = score_L * score_R    # fires on music-AND-plural — a soft conjunction
The whole trick: a product of two linear reads is a degree-2 polynomial in the input. Polynomials, unlike thresholds, can be rewritten in closed form. Hold onto that.

The two real layers of bilin18

Bilinear attention — how tokens talk
# two score circuits, multiplied. no softmax.
pattern = (q1·k1)(q2·k2) / d²   # attend when BOTH agree
out_i   = Σ_j pattern[i,j]·(W_o W_v·x_j)
Two query/key circuits multiplied (a conjunction), then the value/output path delivers the payload. No exponential, no normalization.
Bilinear MLP — how a token thinks
# two linear reads of the residual, multiplied
h   = (L·x) ⊙ (R·x)    # elementwise, 4608 units
out = Down · h
Left and Right read the residual, multiply elementwise, Down writes it back — the single unit, stacked 4,608 wide.
Every layer is multilinear (degree 2–3), except RMS-norm, which at layer 0 is a fixed per-token rescale. That property is what the whole course exploits.

What "folding" and "decomposition" mean

Folding = collapsing a layer's weight matrices into the one interaction tensor they secretly are, exactly, by algebra (Lesson 1). Decomposition = rewriting that tensor in a basis where it becomes a few interpretable pieces, if it can (Lessons 2–4); reading the thin channels between layers when it can't (Lessons 5–6).
weights (L, R, Down)  ──fold──▶  one tensor T[o,i,j]  ──decompose──▶  a few named features
                                  (exact · Lesson 1)     (metric dictionary · Lesson 3)
The plan: fold every layer to a tensor, decompose the ones that will, measure the bonds of the ones that won't. That is the rest of the course.

Tensor-network interpretability · lesson 1

The exact fold

Our layers are bilinear — they multiply two linear reads of the input: out = Dn(L·x ⊙ R·x). Because the product distributes, the three weight matrices are secretly one interaction tensor, and you can fold them into it exactly — an algebraic identity, no information lost, no arbitrary choice added. Every later lesson works on that tensor, so the fold is the entry ticket.

The fold, as a computation you can run

Expand the product and collect the sum over hidden units p. What's left is a third-order tensor T, and out = xᵀ T x term for term. Here it is on the (a+b) mod 23 toy — verified over all 529 inputs:
# out = Dn( (L·x) ⊙ (R·x) )   -- the elementwise product distributes into ONE tensor:
T = einsum('op,pi,pj->oij', Dn, L, R)   # T[o,i,j] = Σ_p Dn[o,p] L[p,i] R[p,j]

layer  = Dn @ ((L @ x) * (R @ x))         # the network's forward pass
folded = einsum('oij,i,j->o', T, x, x)  # the tensor's action
max|layer - folded|  =  1.6e-7 (fp32),  7.4e-16 (fp64)   # an algebraic identity, not a fit

The real thing — two folds inside bilin18

(A) The layer-0 attention fold. At layer 0 the query/key input is the (fixed) normalized embedding, so each head-branch folds in closed form to unit-RMS factor tables of shape (50304, 128); the whole V×V score map is their product, rank ≤ 128 by construction — raw size 884 MiB. Fold-vs-forward gate: 1.3×10⁻¹⁵.
An "exact" claim is a checkable claim. The gate first failed at ~10⁻⁴ — the bug was fp32 rotary tables in the model source, not the algebra. Fixing the source dropped it to 10⁻¹⁵. That is how you earn the word "exact."
(B) The attention head as a third moment. Pile the source side into a tensor C = Σⱼ pⱼ (W1 xⱼ) ⊗ (W2 xⱼ) ⊗ (W_OVᵀ xⱼ); for bilin18, C_ctx = Σⱼ k1ⱼ ⊗ k2ⱼ ⊗ W_o vⱼ ∈ ℝ128×128×1152, and its static component carries ~99% of the delivered energy. Using the exact 128³ moment instead of a sampled estimate (which was ~3/4 noise: 1.084 vs 0.338) improved the compression frontier at equal cost — ΔCE +0.0048 → +0.0027 at 455 Mbit. The fold isn't just pretty; it removes estimator noise.
(C) The MLP fold. bilin18's MLPs are pure bilinear, so each folds by exactly the toy's einsum; the weight-quadratic-form identity gates at 8.3×10⁻⁷ (fp32).
What "no softmax" buys. No softmax means no shift/normalization gauge and exact multilinearity — so gauges can be fully characterized and removed (that's Lesson 2), not that none exist. The one non-polynomial step is RMS-norm, which at layer 0 is a fixed per-token rescale — which is exactly why the layer-0 objects fold in closed form and layer 1 was a separate research decision.

Lesson 2

The gauge trap

The layer is folded (Lesson 1). It is tempting to read its neurons — "neuron 5 fires for X". But the neuron basis is a choice of coordinates, not a fact: you can change every neuron's activation without changing what the layer does. Only the folded tensor is real — and even part of that is invisible.

Same function, totally different neurons

One bilinear layer, one input, drawn two ways. On the right every neuron is rescaled by an arbitrary amount (compensated downstream). Activations move — one swings from about -6.27 to -36.2 — but the output is identical to 1e-14.
a neuron reading
re-gauged — same layer
Permuting or rescaling neurons leaves the folded tensor unchanged to machine precision. So "neuron 5 does X" reads the coordinates, not the layer.

Even the tensor has a hidden half

The tensor takes the input on both legs, so only its symmetric part can affect the output; the antisymmetric part cancels. This layer's tensor is 64% antisymmetric, yet computing from the symmetric part alone changes the output by 2e-14 — a whole chunk that is behaviorally invisible.

The real thing — bilin18

Permuting the neurons of a real bilinear MLP leaves the folded tensor identical to 2.6×10⁻⁷, and ~65% of the raw tensor is antisymmetric gauge — the same phenomenon the toy shows. That is why per-neuron "circuit" stories can be reading noise. There is also a specific attention gauge: the two query/key branches can be swapped and reciprocally rescaled (q1·k1)(q2·k2) is symmetric in them — so we canonicalize (‖W1‖=‖W2‖, fixed order) before any analysis. Findings live only in gauge-invariant quantities — which is why the next lesson goes looking for a basis that is real.

The right basis: how we actually find features

The neurons are a gauge (lesson 2), so we need a basis that's real. Ours is a TopK sparse dictionary fit in the metric the head actually reads — the concatenated read-space [K1·x ; K2·x ; V·x], equivalently whitening by G = W1ᵀW1 + W2ᵀW2 + W_OV W_OVᵀ. TopK, not L1: L1's shrinkage biases the codes, and those codes get cubed in the mechanism core. Here is that exact technique, run on a planted toy so you can watch it recover the features.

The technique, recovering planted features

We plant 12 sparse atoms inside the head-visible subspace, add a loud nuisance living in the head's kernel (a direction the head literally cannot see: M·nuisance = 0), and run the dictionary fit two ways.
# fit a TopK sparse dictionary in the space the HEAD actually reads
# the head sees a token x only through K1x, K2x, Vx  ->  metric G = M^T M
D_metric, code = topk_sae(X @ M.T, K, k)   # OURS: whiten into head-space, then TopK SAE
D_naive , _    = topk_sae(X      , K, k)   # NAIVE L2: fit on raw embeddings

recovery(D_metric) # -> 0.81   # our atoms match the planted features
recovery(D_naive)  # -> 0.13   # naive atoms live in the head's KERNEL (invisible)
atom recovery (cosine to the planted features)
can the head even see the atom? (‖M·atom‖)
Naive L2 doesn't just do "a bit worse" — it spends its atoms on the loud nuisance, so they land in the head's kernel (visibility 0.26, ≈0): it recovers directions the head is structurally blind to. Fit in the head's own geometry and the planted features fall out (0.81). The geometry you measure error in decides what you find.

Run this technique on a real head and you get a small, named dictionary — and it's far cheaper than the alternatives at equal description length.

Tensor-network interpretability · lesson 4

Minimal circuits — and the honest limit

In Lesson 3 we earned a basis that survives the gauge — the metric-aligned sparse dictionary. Now the payoff question: how few of its features actually reproduce the behavior? Sometimes a handful. Sometimes none.

L1 weights fold to interaction tensors · L2 their neurons are an arbitrary gauge · L3 refit in the head's metric → a real sparse dictionary · L4 how few of those atoms suffice — and is any basis special at all?

First: what does "k" mean?

Picture 24 measurements per animal — weight, ear length, tail length, fur density, … It looks 24-dimensional. But suppose every animal is really set by two hidden knobs, "cuteness" and "furriness", and all 24 columns are noisy echoes of those two. Then the data's true k = 2: two features regenerate every column. k is the number of hidden knobs you actually need. The catch is which two — pick cuteness & furriness and you reproduce everything; pick two random blends of the 24 columns and you get almost nothing. That gap is the whole lesson.

Measuring k, with the control that keeps it honest

# given L3's real basis, rank its directions by the MAP, keep k, measure behavior
def chosen(M, k):  # the privileged basis: top-k singular directions of the map
    U, S, Vt = svd(M); return Vt[:k]
def random_(k):    # the CONTROL: k random orthonormal directions
    Q, _ = qr(randn(d, d)); return Q[:k]

chosen_r2 = [r2(X, Y, chosen(M, k))  for k in ks]   # keep the best k features
random_r2 = [r2(X, Y, random_(k))    for k in ks]   # keep k random ones
# chosen >> random  ->  a minimal circuit exists.   chosen == random  ->  distributed.

A minimal sufficient description is not a minimal necessary mechanism.
The tell. Reporting the random-vs-chosen control is what keeps "we found the circuit" honest: chosen ≫ random means a real minimal circuit (mlp16, rank-16); chosen ≈ random means distributed (block-0). Both are true findings — the second just says the honest thing.

Tensor-network interpretability · lesson 5

Communication channels — how wide is the wire?

Zoom out from single nodes to how they talk. Information between two components flows on a bond, and a bond's width is its rank — the number of independent directions that actually cross the cut. Rank is gauge-invariant; it is not the same as how "dense" the matrix looks.

"If it's a rank-16 thing, is it doing something? Isn't that just a dense rotation matrix?" No — those are opposite things. Density is about entries (and is a gauge: it depends on the basis you drew the matrix in). A rotation is dense in entries yet full-rank: every dimension crosses, nothing is lost, it just relabels coordinates — a wide, lossless channel. Rank is about dimensions (gauge-invariant): a rank-16 map projects — 16 directions survive, the rest are destroyed, so the receiver can only ever see 16 numbers. So rank-16 is doing something real — 16 dimensions of it — but through a bottleneck, unlike a rotation which compresses nothing. Your eye can't tell the two matrices apart; the singular-value spectrum can, instantly.

Two bonds your eye can't tell apart

Left: a full-rank rotation. Right: a rank-4 map, dressed with rotations so its entries look just as dense. Same visual blob — but the spectra (below each) give it away: 12 flat values vs 4-then-zero.

The proof: truncate the wire and watch behavior

Keep only the top-r directions of each bond and measure how much of its output survives. The rotation degrades from the very first cut — every dimension was load-bearing (wide). The projection is flat at 1.0 down to r=4, then cliffs — only 4 dimensions ever mattered (narrow).
rotation (wide, lossless)rank-4 projection (narrow, lossy)
A rank-16 bond is real work through a bottleneck. And measure rank on what actually flows, not on the weights: the same map can look wide in weight-space and be narrow on real data.

The real thing — a dense node with a rank-10 bond

Lesson 6

What the bond carries

Lesson 5 counted how many dimensions flow. But a bond can be wide and still sparse — width and sparsity are different axes. A thin channel takes one of three shapes, and each is legible even when the node isn't.

1 · A sparse code — few active symbols of a large dictionary

The wire holds a dictionary of 48 named symbols, but each input lights only a handful. Behavior saturates once ~4 are allowed. This is not the same as narrow: the wire is 48 wide but 4-sparse.
You read the channel as "which few symbols fired" — the way we read attention as a small dictionary of topics and morphology.

2 · A typed blob — an opaque payload, routed unopened

Sometimes the payload is dense and illegible, but the channel is still understood because the payload is typed and routed: carried through intermediate nodes that address it without reading it, opened only at the end. You understand the wire by its routing protocol, not its bytes.

The real thing — bilin18

Sparse code beats narrow at equal bits. Layer-0 attention: a dictionary of n=1024 symbols, k=8 active, costs +0.006 nats at 6.1% of the raw bits — versus SVD rank-16 (a narrow dense bond) at +0.035, 6× worse. Wide-and-sparse wins. The symbols are nameable (music, film, plural-suffix, {the}), and per head the inventory spans 32 to 4,096 symbols — channel vocabulary is a per-component property.
The typed blob — the memory pipeline. A first-mention entity ("Lindsay → Lohan") is carried as a payload through four verified stages: front MLPs write it into the key's residual (block 1 largest, recovery 0.50; context-bound — transplant fails 0.04 vs 1.00); shallow attention layers 3–8 route it (band 0.90, no single layer >0.26 — moved, unopened); it is fetched late (corrupting layers 13–17 of the key destroys 0.99 of the prediction); then read out. The channel is legible (four causal stages); the payload contents are not.
Honest limit: one shared code for every wire fails — a single 512-symbol dictionary coding all bonds costs +0.59 nats. Channels have per-bond vocabularies; there is no universal code.

Two ways to understand a computation

The thesis. For every piece you ask two questions — is the node decomposable? and is the bond sparse? — and a computation is legible if either answer is favorable. A decomposable node you understand from the inside; a dense node with thin bonds you understand entirely from its interface.
